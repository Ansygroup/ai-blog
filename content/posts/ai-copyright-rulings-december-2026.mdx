## The first appeals court ruling

On 29 September 2026 the US Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit published its opinion in Thomson Reuters v. Ross, according to [Legal 500](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/united-states/intellectual-property/third-circuit-ruling-in-ai-copyright-lawsuit-rejects-fair-use-defense) (24 November 2026). It affirmed the district court's partial summary judgment for Thomson Reuters, recognizing its work as copyrightable and finding that a competitor's use of it for AI training was not fair use. Legal 500 describes it as apparently the first decision on this issue at the federal appellate level.

Law.com's analysis of 24 November says the court left open whether AI generated works that compete with but do not copy original content can count as legal harm under copyright law. That limit matters, because the ruling concerned a competing legal research tool, not a general chatbot.

## The big author case

The [Authors Guild reported](https://authorsguild.org/news/final-briefs-in-authors-guild-v-openai/) on 24 November that its case against OpenAI and Microsoft has finished summary judgment briefing. The timeline it gives: the opening motion on 4 September, oppositions on 23 and 24 October, 52 friend of the court briefs on 30 October and final replies on 20 November. The case is part of a larger proceeding, In re OpenAI Copyright Infringement Litigation, which also includes the New York Times and other news plaintiffs.

The Guild says there is no factual dispute that OpenAI downloaded millions of books from pirate sites and trained on many of them. The open question is fair use. The Guild argues there were three separate acts: downloading, training and later use, and that each must be judged on its own. OpenAI and Microsoft argue the purpose is training and that this is transformative, leaning on two 2025 California decisions, Bartz and Kadrey. The Guild expects a hearing to be set and a decision in 2027. That is the Guild's account of its own case, and the defendants' filings were not available to us.

The Authors Guild also quotes the Third Circuit's line from the Ross case that "ease is not a justification," and argues it applies to downloading books from pirate sites because they were free and easy. That is the Guild's reading, and OpenAI and Microsoft dispute the framing. The Ross decision is not binding on the California courts that decided Bartz and Kadrey in 2025, so the question of how these rulings fit together stays open until more appeals courts weigh in.

## Music: from lawsuits to licenses

The Los Angeles Times reported on 21 September 2026 that after the 2024 suits by Universal, Sony and Warner against Suno and Udio, settlements began in 2025. Universal settled with Udio in October and Warner settled with both Udio and Suno in November. In September 2026 Suno released its first models built with licensed music from Warner Music Group, BMG and Believe. Artists on participating labels can opt in and are credited and paid.

## What could go wrong

The common mistake is treating one ruling as settled law for all AI training. The Third Circuit decision concerned a competing product in one circuit. Another mistake is quoting a decision date: nothing in the OpenAI case has been decided.

## Who should skip it

Skip the case detail if you only use AI tools. If you publish AI generated work commercially, read the terms of each tool, since licensing deals change what vendors allow.

## Test it yourself

1. Find the court's own docket or opinion for any ruling you read about.
2. Check the date and which court issued it.
3. Look for whether it is a final ruling or a summary judgment step.
4. Check whether the facts match your use.
5. Ignore any article that predicts the outcome without citing a filing.
