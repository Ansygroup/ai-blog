A newsletter does not die from a lack of ideas. It dies in week nine, when the writer is tired, the open rate is flat and the Sunday deadline feels like homework. AI tools are most useful for that week: they cut the hours spent on research notes, subject lines and reformatting, so the writer's energy goes to the one paragraph only they can write.

## beehiiv: the platform with the clearest money terms

beehiiv's page lists a free plan at $0 and paid tiers at $49 and $95 per month. The detail worth noticing is that beehiiv states it takes 0% of paid subscription revenue. Payments run through Stripe, whose fee on the page read was 2.9% plus $0.30 per charge. So on a $10 monthly subscription, Stripe takes $0.59 and you keep the rest before tax.

That clarity is rare. Many platforms take a percentage on top of payment processing, which compounds as you grow. Do the sum for 500 paid subscribers at $8 a month and a 10% platform cut would cost $400 monthly, which is more than either paid beehiiv tier.

Where it fails: the free plan has limits, and the page lists which features move to paid tiers. Check the subscriber cap and the features you rely on, such as automations and referral programs, before building your list there. Moving a list later is possible, but your archive and links rarely move cleanly.

## Claude for drafts you will rewrite

Claude Pro costs $17 per month billed annually or $20 monthly. The strongest use is not writing the issue. It is the work around it. Paste your rough notes and ask for the three weakest claims. Paste the finished issue and ask for ten subject lines that avoid the words you overuse. Ask it to cut 15% without losing a single fact.

Where it fails: voice. A newsletter is bought for a point of view, and a draft written from nothing reads like everyone else's. If a paragraph could appear in a competitor's issue unchanged, delete it.

## Grammarly for the last pass

Grammarly's paid plan was $12 per month on the page read. It catches typos, repeated words and long sentences. It will also flatten distinctive phrasing, so accept fixes for errors and reject suggestions that change your voice.

## Buffer for distribution

Buffer listed $5 and $10 per channel. After each issue, schedule three posts that quote one idea each, linking to the archive. Distribution is the part most writers skip, and a few scheduled posts per issue is the lowest-effort fix.

## What the vendor pages do not say

Open rates are less reliable than they were. Mail apps that preload images inflate opens, so a subject line test based on opens alone can reward the wrong winner. Use clicks and replies as your measure instead. A newsletter with a 40% open rate and no replies is weaker than one with 20% and ten replies each week.

A second hidden cost is time on the list. Growing a list beyond your first 1,000 readers usually comes from one source: recommendations from other newsletters or consistent posts on one social channel. Pick one and do it for 12 weeks before adding tools.

## A weekly workflow that fits in three hours

Monday: collect links and notes in one document as you read, with a single sentence on why each matters. Wednesday: pick the one idea you have the strongest opinion on and write 400 words badly and fast. Thursday: ask Claude for the weak spots, rewrite by hand, and run the grammar pass. Friday: schedule the issue and the three social posts. The point is a fixed rhythm. Writers who publish on the same weekday for six months keep more readers than writers who publish brilliant issues at random intervals.

## Who should skip this list

If you write fewer than twice a month, any free tool works and optimizing is premature. If your newsletter is a company update to existing customers, your CRM's email tool probably already does the job.
