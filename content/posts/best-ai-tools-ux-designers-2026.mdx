Design work has always been two jobs: making the thing and convincing people the thing is right. AI tools have become good at the second job faster than the first, which is why the most useful purchases for a UX designer in 2026 are often not design tools at all. They are the ones that turn a messy research session into a decision a stakeholder can follow.

Prices were read on vendor pricing pages on November 27, 2026. Seat pricing on design tools changes with bundle structure, so confirm before you quote a project rate.

## The short answer

| Scenario | Pick | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interface design and handoff | Figma | Still the shared file everyone expects |
| Marketing pages and portfolio | Framer | Publish without an engineer |
| Sitemaps and wireframe scaffolding | Relume | Turns a brief into structure fast |
| Research synthesis | Claude or ChatGPT | Fast themes, needs your transcript |
| Usability testing at small scale | Maze | Structured tasks without a lab |

## What AI is actually good at in UX

Three things, and none of them is visual design.

It is good at synthesis: reading twelve interview transcripts and grouping the complaints into themes you then verify by re-reading the quotes. It is good at variation: producing eight versions of a microcopy block or an error state. And it is good at the artifact nobody wants to make: the competitive teardown, the heuristic review write-up, the accessibility checklist.

It is bad at the thing designers are actually paid for, which is knowing which problem to solve. A model will happily generate a beautiful flow for a feature nobody needs.

## Figma: the file everyone opens

Figma's seat prices start at $3 a month for a collaboration seat and $16 for a full design seat, rising to $55 and $90 at the organization and enterprise tiers.

The reason it stays central is not features, it is convention. Clients, engineers, and product managers all have a Figma account, and a design that lives anywhere else creates friction at exactly the moment you need approval.

The cost trap is seats. A team that adds every stakeholder as a full seat is paying five times what a collaboration seat would cost for people who only need to comment.

## Framer: shipping without an engineer

Framer lists Basic at $10 a month and Pro at $30, with additional editor seats at $20 and $10. For a designer with a portfolio or a small marketing site, it removes the handoff entirely: you design and publish in the same place.

The limitation is complexity. A marketing page with animation and CMS content fits well. A product interface with authentication and state does not, and pretending otherwise costs more than hiring a developer for a week.

## Relume: structure before pixels

Relume's pricing is split between the AI workspace and hosting: Launch hosting is $12 per site a month billed yearly or $16 monthly, and the Pro AI workspace plan starts at $24 a month.

Its value is the step designers skip and later regret: sitemap and section-level structure. Generating a first-pass sitemap from a brief is a genuinely useful way to start a client conversation, because it makes assumptions visible early.

The failure mode is sameness. Generated sitemaps converge on the same section order, which is fine for a marketing site and wrong for anything with an unusual content model.

## Maze: testing without a lab

Maze is the practical option for unmoderated testing: you build a task, send a link, and get results without scheduling sessions. Pricing is quote-based, which means the sales call is part of the evaluation.

The AI features help with analysis, but the value is the volume. Five quick tests with real users beat one polished study that takes three weeks to recruit.

## Claude and ChatGPT: the research layer

The most valuable use in a designer's week is turning transcripts into themes with quotes attached, then writing the summary that goes to stakeholders. Both models do this well, and both will invent a quote if you ask for one without providing the transcript.

The rule that protects you: paste the actual transcript, ask for themes with verbatim quotes, and check every quote against the source. A fabricated participant quote in a research readout is a career risk, not a typo.

The second use is accessibility: generating alt text drafts, checking reading level, and producing a checklist for a component audit. All of it needs a human pass, and all of it saves an hour.

## Where UX teams get this wrong

The first mistake is synthetic users. A model role-playing a persona is not user research, and treating it as a substitute produces confident decisions with no evidence behind them.

The second is AI-generated UI shipped without a state audit. Generated screens look complete and are usually missing empty states, errors, and loading behavior, which is where real products break.

The third is the handoff gap. A design generated quickly is harder for an engineer to build than one designed with constraints in mind, and the cost lands on someone else's deadline.

## Who should skip this list

Skip Relume if your work is product interfaces rather than marketing sites. Skip Maze if your users are hard to reach and recruiting is the real bottleneck. And skip the generative UI tools entirely if your design system is mature, because the output will not respect it.

## What to buy first

Figma for the file, one model subscription for synthesis and copy, and Framer or Relume depending on whether you ship pages or structures. That is under $60 a month and covers the work that actually slows projects down.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does Figma cost for a design team?

Figma lists collaboration seats from $3 a month and full design seats from $16, rising to $55 and $90 at the organization and enterprise tiers. Most teams overspend by giving stakeholders full seats they never need.

### Can AI replace user research?

No. A model role-playing a persona produces confident assumptions, not evidence. AI is useful for synthesizing real transcripts into themes, provided you check every quote against the source.

### Is Framer a replacement for a developer?

For marketing pages and portfolios, yes. Framer Basic is $10 a month and Pro $30. For product interfaces with authentication and complex state, it is not.
