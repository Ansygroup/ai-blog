A plumber with two vans cannot answer the phone at 11am on a Tuesday. Neither can a two-person law office, a dental clinic with one front-desk hire, or a landscaper on a mower. The call goes to voicemail, the caller dials the next name on the list, and nobody ever learns the job was lost.

An AI receptionist fixes that specific problem for roughly the price of a coffee per hundred calls. Not the whole front desk. Not scheduling strategy. Just the phone, answered every time, with the caller's name, number and reason captured and the appointment booked or the message routed.

This is the build we run for small businesses: a voice agent on a real phone number, five intents, a calendar, a transfer rule, and a hard cost ceiling. Budget 90 minutes for a first version and a week of supervised calls before it goes live unattended.

*Last tested: November 2026.*

## Who this is for, and who should skip it

Build it if you miss calls and cannot hire for them: trades, clinics, salons, small firms, anyone whose revenue arrives by phone. Skip it if your calls are mostly complex negotiations, if you operate in a jurisdiction where recording consent is strict and you will not add a disclosure, or if your calendar is a mess. An AI receptionist pointed at a broken calendar books chaos faster.

## Step 1: Write the five intents before you touch a tool

List every reason people call you, then cut to five. A working set for a service business:

1. Book a new appointment.
2. Reschedule or cancel an existing one.
3. Ask a price or availability question.
4. Reach a specific person.
5. Anything else, which becomes a message.

Anything outside the five gets a message and a promise of a callback. Resist adding intents in week one. Each one adds a branch the agent can get wrong, and a wrong answer on a price question costs more trust than a missed call.

The bundled model is simpler and usually cheaper to start. The unbundled model is cheaper at volume if you already have wholesale model and telephony rates, and more work to wire up.

## Step 3: Understand what a minute actually costs

Retell publishes a worked example that adds up to about $0.11 per minute: $0.04 for the language model, $0.055 for voice infrastructure, $0.015 for text to speech, and nothing for telephony. Swap in a premium voice and the number moves. Retell lists ElevenLabs Flash at $0.04 per minute, Multilingual v2 at $0.06, and v3 at $0.10, so a nicer voice can add more than the platform fee itself.

Run your own arithmetic before you build. A business taking 400 calls a month at 2.5 minutes each is 1,000 minutes, which is about $110 a month at $0.11, before the phone number. That is the number to compare against an answering service, not against a salary.

Two cost traps worth knowing. First, failed and abandoned calls still burn minutes, so a bad prompt costs money every time it mishears. Second, premium voices plus long greetings plus a chatty agent multiply: the same 1,000 minutes at $0.20 lands at $200.

## Step 4: Write the script as constraints, not prose

Give the agent rules, not a paragraph to recite. This skeleton works:

```
You are the receptionist for [business]. You answer calls, book appointments
and take messages.

Rules:
- Keep answers under 25 words. Never guess a price. If asked for an exact
  price, say you will have someone confirm and take a number.
- Collect name, phone number and reason before ending any call.
- Never promise a time that is not offered by the booking tool.
- If the caller asks for a human, transfer immediately. Do not ask why.
- If you do not understand twice, offer a callback and end politely.
```

The "never guess a price" line is the one that saves you. Voice models will improvise a quote if the caller pushes, and a wrong quote becomes an argument at the counter.

## Step 5: Connect the calendar, not just the transcript

A receptionist that takes messages is a voicemail with better manners. The value is in the booking: the agent reads real availability and writes the appointment back. Connect the calendar account the business actually uses, restrict the agent to open slots, and set a buffer between jobs.

Test the write path first. Book, then check the calendar, then cancel through the agent. If cancellation does not free the slot, stop and fix it before going live.

## Step 6: Build the transfer rule and the fallback

Every call needs three exits: booked, messaged, or transferred to a human. Set a transfer number that a person actually answers during business hours, and after hours route to a message.

Then decide what happens when the platform is down or the caller has terrible reception. The answer should be a plain voicemail on the same number, not a dead line. Test it by disabling the agent.

## Step 7: Supervise 20 real calls before trusting it

Record and read the transcripts of the first 20 calls. Score them on three things: did it get the name and number, did it book the right slot, did it say anything untrue. Expect two or three bad calls in that batch. Fix the prompt, not the model.

Add a daily transcript review for the first two weeks. Most failures are one of four kinds: misheard names, interruptions, callers who talk over the greeting, and questions that should have been transferred.

## What can go wrong

**Recording disclosure.** Some jurisdictions require all parties to consent to recording. Put the disclosure in the greeting, not in a policy page.

**Latency.** A voice agent that pauses two seconds before every reply feels broken. Shorter prompts and a faster voice model fix more of this than any settings toggle.

**Accents and names.** Expect the agent to mangle unusual names. Ask callers to spell, and read the number back.

**Spam and robocalls.** Real phone numbers attract junk. A short screen at the start of the call, or a blocklist on the number provider, keeps minutes from leaking.

**The agent inventing policy.** "Yes, we take returns after 60 days" is the kind of sentence that costs real money. Constrain the agent to a written policy list, and say "I will have someone confirm" for anything else.

## The judgment call

Build this if the phone is a real revenue channel and you are losing calls you never see. Do not build it to avoid hiring a receptionist you need anyway: a good front-desk person handles the exceptions, and the exceptions are where the money is. The realistic version is a hybrid. The agent covers overflow, after hours and the calls nobody wants to answer, and a human covers everything that matters.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does an AI receptionist cost per call?

On Retell's published example the stack adds up to about $0.11 per minute, so a 2.5-minute call costs roughly 28 cents before the phone number. Vapi charges $0.05 per minute for hosting plus your own model, voice and telephony costs.

### Can an AI receptionist book appointments directly into my calendar?

Yes, if the platform supports calendar tools and you connect the account the business actually uses. Test the write path by booking, checking the calendar, then cancelling through the agent before you go live.

### Do I have to tell callers they are talking to an AI?

Several jurisdictions require disclosure, and some require all-party consent for recording. Put a one-line disclosure in the greeting rather than relying on a policy page.
