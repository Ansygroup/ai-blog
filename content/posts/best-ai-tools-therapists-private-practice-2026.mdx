A therapist in private practice has two jobs that compete for the same hours: clinical work and running a business. The clinical hour is the one they trained for. The other one, which is scheduling, notes, insurance, and the awkward conversation about a late cancellation fee, is where the burnout comes from. That is the part worth automating, with limits that are stricter than in most other professions.

## The rule that comes before every tool choice

Client information is protected health information in most jurisdictions, and a consumer chatbot is not a covered entity or a business associate. That means no names, no dates of birth, no session content pasted into a free tool, no matter how convenient.

The workable pattern is to de-identify: remove names and identifying details, use the model for structure and language, then put the specifics back yourself in your own system. It is slower than pasting a transcript, and it is the only version that survives an audit.

## SimplePractice: the all-in-one default

SimplePractice lists plans at $49, $79, and $99 a month, with promotional first-year pricing for new subscribers and additional practitioners at $37 a month on the multi-clinician plan.

The value is consolidation. Notes, scheduling, insurance claims, and a client portal in one place means one login and one source of truth, which for a solo practitioner is worth more than any single feature. The tiers differ mainly in insurance billing and team features, so a cash-pay solo practice can often stay on the entry tier.

The limitation is flexibility. Clinicians with a niche workflow, such as intensive programs or group practices with complex billing, often find the structure constraining.

## Jane: lighter, with an AI scribe

Jane lists plans at $59, $79, and $99 a month, with AI Scribe at $15 per practitioner a month for unlimited notes, five free notes a month on the base plans, and insurance billing at $20 a month plus $5 per license.

The AI scribe is the feature that changes a therapist's evening. A drafted note saves fifteen minutes per session, which for a caseload of twenty is five hours a week. The non-negotiable condition is that you read every note before signing, because a drafted note that misstates what a client said is a clinical record error, not a typo.

## Scheduling and the no-show problem

Online booking and reminders are the highest-return automation in a private practice. Calendly's paid plans are listed at $10 and $16 a month if your platform does not include booking, though most practice platforms now do.

The policy matters more than the tool. A clear cancellation window, stated in the intake paperwork and enforced consistently, prevents more lost revenue than any reminder sequence. Automation makes a vague policy worse, because it applies it uniformly and without judgment.

## Where AI helps with the business side

Three places, safely.

Writing the practice's website copy and the plain-language explanation of what therapy involves, which is genuinely hard to write well. Drafting insurance appeal letters, where the structure is repetitive and the clinical content is yours. And summarizing your own process notes for supervision, with identifiers removed.

The failure mode to watch for is therapeutic language in administrative writing. A model asked to write a cancellation policy will produce something warm and vague. You want something clear and kind, and those are different.

## Where therapists get this wrong

The first mistake is a consumer AI tool with client data in it. The second is an AI scribe whose recordings live on a vendor server with no business associate agreement.

The third is over-automation of the therapeutic relationship: automated check-in messages, AI-generated responses to client emails, sentiment analysis of session notes. Clients notice, and the therapeutic alliance is the treatment.

## Group practices and supervision

A group practice has one problem a solo clinician does not: consistency. Two therapists writing notes in two styles creates a record that is hard to audit and harder to hand over. A shared note template, kept in the practice platform, fixes most of it.

Supervision is the other edge case. Sharing a drafted summary with a supervisor is useful, and it is also a disclosure of client material. Keep supervision documents inside the practice's own systems, and treat any AI tool used for them with the same rules as a clinical record.

## Who should skip this list

Skip a new practice platform if you are mid-treatment with a full caseload and your current system works, because migration is a clinical risk. Skip AI scribes if your notes are already brief and you would not review a draft carefully. And skip any tool that cannot show you where the data lives.

## What to buy first

A practice platform with notes, scheduling, and a portal. Then, if the platform offers a compliant scribe, add it and read every note. Everything else, from website copy to appeal letters, can be done with a de-identified draft and your own review.
