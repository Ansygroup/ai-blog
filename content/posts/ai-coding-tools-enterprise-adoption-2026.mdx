Last checked: November 2026. Sources: the JetBrains Developer Ecosystem Survey 2026 (more than 15,000 professional developers, fieldwork May to July 2026, [JetBrains blog, 18 August 2026](https://blog.jetbrains.com/research/2026/08/ai-coding-agent-adoption-2026/)), Stack Overflow's 2026 Developer Survey (over 30,000 responses, [press release, 6 October 2026](https://stackoverflow.co/company/press/archive/stack-overflow-annual-developer-survey-ai-trust-conditional/)), and the public pricing pages of Cursor and GitHub Copilot. Both surveys are self-reported, and JetBrains reweights its sample to represent the global developer population.

AI coding tools are no longer an experiment that a few engineers run on the side. The open question for a company is no longer whether developers use them. It is which tools, under what policy, and at what cost.

## How many developers use them

| Measure | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Professional developers using AI coding agents at work at least weekly | 90% | JetBrains, May to July 2026 |
| Using them daily | 68% | JetBrains, May to July 2026 |
| Respondents using coding assistants or coding agents | 66% | Stack Overflow, 2026 |
| Respondents who do not use AI tools | 17.2% | Stack Overflow, 2026 |
| Respondents using automated agent workflows | 26.2% | Stack Overflow, 2026 |

The two surveys ask different questions of different samples, so the 90% and the 66% are not in conflict. JetBrains counts weekly use of agents among professional developers. Stack Overflow counts a broader group of technologists and a broader answer set. Quote one or the other, with its definition, never an average of both.

## The governance gap

Here is what no vendor page tells you. Stack Overflow found that 30% of developers say AI use at work is optional and left to the individual, while only 24% say their organisation publishes approved tools or an official AI policy. Usage has outrun policy. If you manage engineers, the chances are good that some of them are already using a tool you have not approved.

Trust is conditional. The same survey says nearly half of respondents trust AI output when they can verify it, and only 6.6% are prepared to trust it for important decisions. Developers who switched tools mostly did so because the output was better (25%), and 11% because their organisation required it.

## What the paid plans look like

Pricing changes often, so confirm on the vendor page. As of our November 2026 check of the public pages:

- **GitHub Copilot:** Free at $0, Pro at $10 per month, Pro+ at $39, Max at $100. Each plan includes a monthly allowance of GitHub AI Credits, where one credit is $0.01. Code completions do not use credits, while chat and agent work do. On Business and Enterprise, admins set usage limits and decide whether extra paid usage is allowed. We did not capture the Business and Enterprise prices.
- **Cursor:** Free, then individual tiers from $8 to $200 per month, Teams from $30 per user, and Enterprise with custom pricing.

The shift to credit-based billing matters for budgeting. A flat seat price no longer fixes the bill when agent sessions run long. Ask your vendor how overage is capped before you roll out to a team. If you are also weighing chat products for the wider company, see [ChatGPT Team vs Enterprise](/posts/chatgpt-team-vs-enterprise-which-openai-plan-is-right-for-yo).

## A rollout checklist

1. Publish an approved tool list and a one-page policy. Only 24% of organisations do, so this puts you ahead.
2. Set a spending cap per developer, and an alert threshold.
3. Require review of agent-written changes by a person, in line with the verify-first attitude developers already report.
4. Decide what code and data may be sent to which vendor.
5. Measure something other than usage: review time, defects found after release, and time to merge.

## What to watch

As of November 2026, open questions rather than predictions: whether Copilot's share keeps falling, whether Codex's growth continues, how often teams hit credit limits, and whether more organisations publish AI policies.

## Our judgment

The adoption question is settled, and the management question is not. If your engineers are among the 90% using agents weekly, a tool ban is unlikely to hold, and an approved list with spending limits will serve you better. Pick one or two tools, pilot them for a month with real metrics, and treat survey share as a reason to include a tool in the pilot, not as proof it is the best fit for your codebase.
