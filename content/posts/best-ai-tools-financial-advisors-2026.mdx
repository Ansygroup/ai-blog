An advisor's time splits three ways: meeting clients, preparing for meetings, and documenting what was said. The third one is where the liability lives and where AI is most tempting. A tool that drafts a meeting summary in ninety seconds looks like a gift until the summary omits the sentence where the client said they did not want to sell.

Prices were read on vendor pricing pages on November 27, 2026. Advisory technology is usually quoted, so the figures below come from the CRM and productivity layer rather than portfolio systems.

## The compliance frame comes first

Before any tool choice, three rules shape what is possible. Client data stays in systems the firm controls, so a consumer chat window is not a place to paste a portfolio. Marketing output is regulated, which means testimonials, performance claims, and hypothetical illustrations carry rules that a model does not know. And records have to be retained, which means the tool that produces a client communication needs an archive.

An advisor who gets those three right can use almost any tool on this list safely. One who gets them wrong can create a regulatory problem with a $12 subscription.

## Wealthbox: the CRM with transparent pricing

Wealthbox lists plans at $65, $85, and $119 per user a month, with lower add-on tiers at $49 and $29. It is designed around households and activities rather than tickets and deals, which is the right shape for advisory work: who did I speak to, about what, and when is the next contact due.

The feature that earns the fee is the workflow: a new client triggers a sequence of tasks that someone can see and complete. Most advisory practices lose more to forgotten follow-ups than to bad advice.

The limitation is depth on the investment side. Portfolio data, rebalancing, and performance reporting live in other systems, and the CRM will not replace them.

## Redtail: the incumbent that is hard to leave

Redtail lists $39 per user a month billed yearly or $45 monthly, with a higher tier at $59 and $65. It is the system a large share of independent practices already run.

The honest advice is boring: if it works, keep it. Migrating a CRM mid-year costs weeks of attention and loses the activity history that makes the tool valuable. Switch when you are consolidating systems, not because a competitor's interface looks nicer.

## HubSpot: when growth is the problem

HubSpot's CRM is free, with Starter at $7 per seat a month and the next tier at $20. For an advisor trying to build a pipeline of referrals and seminar leads, the marketing side is the draw: landing pages, email sequences, and a form that drops a prospect into a pipeline.

The compliance catch is that HubSpot makes it easy to publish and send at scale. Any performance claim or client story in a campaign needs review before it goes out, and the tool will not warn you.

## Claude and ChatGPT: the preparation layer

The genuinely valuable use is meeting preparation. Feed in the last three meeting notes and the client's stated goals, and ask for a one-page agenda with the questions you should ask. That is fifteen minutes saved per meeting, and the quality of the meeting improves because the agenda is specific.

The second use is the follow-up letter. A model can turn your notes into a clear summary of what was discussed and what happens next, which clients appreciate and which regulators like to see.

The failure mode is the invented fact: a contribution limit, a tax threshold, a deadline. Those numbers change annually and a model's memory of them is not a source. If a number matters, it comes from the current rule, not from the draft.

## Meeting transcription: the highest-risk convenience

Recording client meetings and generating notes is now standard in the industry, and it is genuinely useful. It is also the place where a firm's compliance posture is most exposed.

Three questions before turning it on: does the client consent, where is the audio stored, and does the retention period match the firm's books and records obligation. A tool that keeps transcripts on a vendor's servers indefinitely is a problem waiting for an exam.

## Where advisors get this wrong

The first mistake is using a free chat tool for client data. The second is letting a model write anything that reads as a recommendation. The third is trusting a generated summary without reading it, which is the same failure as signing a letter you did not read.

There is a fourth that is easy to miss: consistency. If two clients in similar situations get letters written by two different models on two different days, the advice can drift in tone and substance. Standardize the prompt and the template.

## Who should skip this list

Skip a new CRM if your current one holds five years of activity and works. Skip AI transcription if your clients are uncomfortable being recorded, which is more common than vendors assume. And skip any marketing automation until your compliance review process is documented, because scale amplifies whatever is wrong.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does Wealthbox cost?

Wealthbox lists plans at $65, $85 and $119 per user a month, with lower add-on tiers at $49 and $29. Redtail, the common alternative, lists $39 per user a month billed yearly or $45 monthly.

### Can financial advisors use ChatGPT with client data?

Not in a consumer chat window. Client data should stay in systems the firm controls, with the retention and access rules your compliance process requires. Use models for drafting from material you are permitted to process.

### Is AI meeting transcription allowed for client meetings?

Usually yes, with consent, controlled storage and a retention period that matches your books and records obligation. The risk is transcripts sitting on a vendor server with no defined deletion.
