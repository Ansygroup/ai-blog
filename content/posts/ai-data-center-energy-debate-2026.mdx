Last checked: November 2026. Figures below come from the International Energy Agency (IEA), mainly its April 2026 report "Key Questions on Energy and AI" and its AI topic page. Where a number is from April 2026, it says so.

The short version: each AI task is getting cheaper in energy terms, and total demand is still rising fast, because far more people use AI and the heaviest uses, such as agents, are growing. Both statements are true at once, and most of the public argument comes from people quoting only one of them.

The IEA's World Energy Outlook 2026 puts a number on the doubling: data centre electricity reaching about 1,000 TWh by 2030 in its Stated Policies scenario ([IEA overview and key findings](https://www.iea.org/reports/world-energy-outlook-2026/overview-and-key-findings)). That is a scenario, not a forecast of what will happen.

## Why both sides can be right

The IEA says power use per AI task is falling quickly, with efficiency improving at a rate it calls unprecedented in energy history. A single chat answer costs less energy than it did a year or two ago.

Total use still climbs. The same report points to more users and to energy-intensive uses, with AI agents named as an example. An agent that runs dozens of model calls to finish one job uses far more power than one reply. If you have read about [how AI agents differ from chatbots](/posts/ai-agents-vs-chatbots-plain-english-2026), that difference is the reason the aggregate numbers keep rising even as per-call efficiency improves.

## The scale of one site

The IEA's AI topic page gives a useful comparison. Traditional data centres draw roughly 10 to 25 MW. Hyperscale AI data centres can exceed 100 MW, which it equates to the yearly consumption of 100,000 households. Source: [IEA, Artificial Intelligence topic page](https://www.iea.org/topics/artificial-intelligence).

That is why the argument is local. A national total is abstract. A 100 MW load arriving in one county, needing new generation and grid upgrades, is something residents and utilities notice.

## The bottleneck is not only electricity

The April 2026 report says the limit on near-term growth is physical. Supply chains for gas turbines, transformers, advanced chips and other components have tightened. A large pipeline of projects is also straining planning and permitting systems, which delays grid connections.

Because grid connections are slow, developers are advancing many projects with onsite natural gas generation, mostly in the United States. The IEA's satellite tracking shows many of these are still early stage. It also notes a technical problem: AI data centres have rapid, large swings in demand, which onsite gas plants can struggle to follow, so onsite batteries are part of the answer.

## Prices and affordability

The report's finding on household bills is conditional. With the right policy and infrastructure investment, higher electricity demand does not necessarily raise prices. But data centres are a special case, because their loads are large, concentrated and scale up fast, which often forces new generation and grid investment. That cost allocation question, who pays for the new capacity, is where most local disputes sit.

## Clean power, or more fossil fuel?

Tech companies signed about 40% of corporate renewable power purchase agreements in 2025 and are driving interest in nuclear and advanced geothermal. The small modular reactor pipeline of conditional agreements grew from 25 GW to 45 GW. "Conditional offtake" means a buyer has agreed to take power if a reactor gets built. It is not electricity on the grid. Treat those 45 GW as intent, and expect a long gap before any of it delivers.

## What you will not find on a vendor page

Efficiency claims and total-demand claims use different denominators. When a company says a model uses a fraction of the energy per query it used before, that can be accurate and still say nothing about its total footprint. Ask two questions of any statement: per what, and compared with when? A per-task figure going down next to a company-wide figure going up is the normal pattern right now, not a contradiction.

## What to watch

- Whether the IEA's planned platform for governments and industry, which it said it would launch soon, publishes regular data.
- Whether onsite gas projects move from early stage to operating, since the IEA's tracking shows most have not yet.
- Whether regulators change who pays for grid upgrades triggered by large loads.
- Whether agent-heavy workloads, such as those in [the 2026 agent frameworks](/news/ai-agents-2026-manus-n8n-langgraph-crewai-explained), push usage growth past per-task savings.

These are open questions as of November 2026, not predictions.

## Our judgment

Do not pick a side from a single statistic. The efficiency gains are real, and so is a 17% rise in data centre demand in one year against 3% for the whole grid. The honest position is that AI's energy footprint is a local infrastructure problem, about turbines, transformers and permits, more than a global climate headline. If you buy AI services, expect providers to keep citing per-task efficiency while their total consumption grows, and judge them by the second number as well.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much did data centre electricity demand grow in 2025?

17%, according to the IEA's April 2026 report, compared with 3% growth in global electricity demand.

### Is AI getting more energy efficient?

Per task, yes, and quickly according to the IEA. Total consumption still rises because usage grows and agents run many calls per job.

### How big is a hyperscale AI data centre?

The IEA says they can exceed 100 MW, about the yearly use of 100,000 households, against 10 to 25 MW for traditional data centres.
