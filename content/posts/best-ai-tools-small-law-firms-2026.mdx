Small law firms have the worst version of the AI problem: the work is perfectly suited to language models, and the professional rules make careless use of them a career event. The firms getting this right in 2026 use AI aggressively for drafting and intake while treating confidentiality and citation-checking as hard walls, not preferences.

## Who this is for, and who should skip it

This is for solo practitioners and firms up to about twenty lawyers doing contract, estate, family, immigration, or small-business work. Skip the consumer chatbots entirely for anything containing client confidences unless you are on business tiers with contractual data protections. Privilege is the whole game; a tool that trains on your client's facts is not a productivity win, it is a bar complaint.

## Drafting and first-pass research: Claude Pro or ChatGPT Plus

For engagement letters, demand letters, client updates, and internal memos, a general chatbot saves small firms hours a week. ChatGPT Plus is $20 a month as of November 2026; Claude Pro is $20 a month or about $17 on annual billing, and Claude's long-document behavior makes it the better pick for forty-page agreements and their redline narratives. Our [ChatGPT tools for lawyers](/posts/best-ai-chatgpt-tools-for-lawyers-2026) guide covers legal prompt patterns in detail.

The failure mode is famous for a reason. In Mata v. Avianca in 2023, two New York lawyers and their firm were sanctioned and ordered to pay $5,000 after filing a brief full of cases ChatGPT had invented. Courts since then have kept sanctioning the same mistake. The rule that protects you: AI output is a draft from a brilliant, unreliable intern. Every case name, citation, and quote gets verified in Westlaw, Lexis, or the court's own site before it appears in anything filed. No exceptions, no matter how plausible the citation looks.

## Contract review and drafting: legal-specific platforms

Purpose-built legal AI (contract review, clause comparison, drafting against your playbook) is where the category has matured fastest. These platforms cost more than a chatbot, typically quote-priced for firms, and earn it through two things consumer tools lack: training on legal corpora rather than the open web, and deployment terms built for privilege. Our comparison of [legal contract analysis platforms](/posts/comparison-ai-powered-legal-contract-analysis-risk-assessment-platforms) and the [legal contract drafting tools](/posts/best-ai-powered-legal-contract-drafting-tools-2026) roundup break down the options and their tradeoffs.

The trap is per-seat math. A platform priced per lawyer per month is a rounding error for a solo and a serious line item for a fifteen-lawyer firm, and vendors know it. Negotiate firm-wide pricing early, and pilot on one practice group before rolling out.

## Research with citations you must still verify: Perplexity Pro

For the non-filing questions that fill a lawyer's day ("what is the statute of limitations posture on X in Y state," "summarize this new regulation"), Perplexity Pro at $17 a month billed annually as of November 2026 returns sourced answers fast. It is the right first stop and the wrong last stop: it cites what is published on the web, not the current state of the statute, and it is not a citator. Nothing it says tells you whether a case is still good law. Use it to orient, then do the real research in the real databases.

## Client intake and the front office

The least glamorous, highest-ROI use in a small firm is intake: a chatbot on the website that answers "do you handle this," collects conflict-check basics, and books consultations. This is marketing and admin, not legal advice, so the confidentiality stakes are lower, and the payoff is measured in signed clients who would otherwise have called the next firm on the list. Keep the bot away from anything resembling legal advice; intake scripts should collect facts and set expectations, never opine.

## What will quietly cost you

Three traps beyond hallucination. First, privilege leakage through convenience: pasting a client email into a consumer chatbot "just to soften the tone" is still a disclosure. Second, billing honesty: if AI cuts a six-hour drafting job to ninety minutes, billing six hours anyway is the kind of thing that unravels in a fee dispute. Third, delegation drift: the model drafts, the associate skims, nobody checks the citations. Write the verification step into your filing checklist so it survives busy weeks.

## How to decide

1. Drowning in drafts and client communication? Start with Claude Pro or ChatGPT Plus at $20 a month, on business tiers if client data is involved.
2. Contracts are the practice? Pilot a legal-specific platform with one practice group.
3. Losing after-hours clients to slow intake? Fix the website chatbot before any research tool.
4. Doing quick orientation research daily? Perplexity Pro, with Westlaw or Lexis as the verifier of record.

The judgment at the end is the same one the sanction orders keep repeating: the model is a drafting accelerator, and the lawyer's name on the filing is the warranty. Buy speed, keep the warranty.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can lawyers use ChatGPT or Claude with client information?

Not on consumer plans. Client confidences belong in business or enterprise tiers with contractual data protections, or in legal-specific platforms built for privilege. Even tone-polishing a client email in a consumer chatbot is a disclosure.

### What happened to lawyers who filed AI-invented cases?

In Mata v. Avianca (2023), two lawyers and their firm were sanctioned and ordered to pay $5,000 after filing a brief of ChatGPT-invented cases. Courts have kept sanctioning the same mistake since. Verify every citation in a real database.

### Is Perplexity good enough for legal research?

As a first stop, yes: Pro is $17/mo billed annually (as of November 2026) and returns sourced answers fast. It is not a citator and cannot tell you if a case is still good law, so verification happens in Westlaw, Lexis, or the court's site.

### What is the highest-ROI AI use for a small firm?

Usually intake, not drafting: a website chatbot that answers basics, collects conflict-check facts, and books consultations captures clients who would otherwise call the next firm on the list.
