Most standard operating procedures are written by the person who already knows the job, for a reader who has never done it, in a document neither of them opens again. The result is a folder of procedures that describe the happy path and skip the four exceptions that actually take the time.

AI fixes the writing speed, which is the smallest part of the problem. The part it can help with, if you feed it the right raw material, is the exceptions: the branches a person only remembers when something goes wrong.

*Last tested: November 2026.*

## Step 1: Record the task instead of writing it

Pick one process that a new hire gets wrong. Record yourself doing it, narrating out loud: what you are clicking, why, what you check, and what you do when something looks off.

Ten minutes of narration produces more usable material than an hour of typing, because you say the exception when you hit it. The narration is the asset. The document is a rendering of it.

## Step 2: Transcribe, then split by decision

Transcribe the recording, then ask the model to separate actions from decisions:

```
Below is a transcript of me doing [process]. Split it into:
1. Steps that always happen, in order.
2. Decision points, with the condition and what I do in each branch.
3. Exceptions I mentioned, with what triggers them.
Quote my wording where it matters. Do not add steps I did not mention.
```

The third list is the one nobody writes down. Every branch you mention casually ("if the invoice is over a thousand I check with finance") becomes a rule a new person can follow.

## Step 3: Structure each step as verb plus object plus check

A step that says "process the order" teaches nothing. A step that says "open the order in the admin panel, check the shipping address against the payment record, then mark as ready" can be followed by someone on their second day.

Rewrite every step to that shape. Then add the check: what tells you the step worked. That single addition turns a checklist into something a person can self-correct with.

## Step 4: Add the four questions every SOP needs

At the end of the document, four headings:

- What if the system is down?
- Who approves an exception?
- What does done look like?
- Who do I ask when this document is wrong?

The last one is the difference between a living document and a dead one. Put a name and a channel, not "the team".

## Step 5: Test it with someone who has never done the job

Hand the document to a person who has not done the task, and watch them follow it without help. Do not answer questions during the test; write them down. Every question is a missing line.

This test takes 20 minutes and finds more problems than any review meeting. It is also the only way to catch the step you skipped because it is obvious to you.

## Step 6: Put the document where the work happens

A SOP in a shared drive is a document nobody reads. The same steps inside the tool, as a checklist on the task, get used. If your work lives in a project tool, paste the steps into a template there.

For recurring processes, a simple automation can create the checklist when a trigger fires. Our guide to [AI automation with Zapier](/posts/ai-automation-with-zapier-2026) covers the pattern.

## Step 7: Set a review date and an owner

Every SOP gets two fields at the top: owner and last reviewed. Six months without a review means the document is probably wrong in at least one place, and a wrong SOP is worse than none because people follow it.

Put the review in the calendar when you publish, not when you remember.

## What can go wrong

**Happy-path-only documents.** The exceptions are the job. If the SOP has no branches, it was written from memory rather than from a recording.

**Screenshots that go stale.** Interfaces change monthly. Use screenshots for orientation and text for the steps, so a redesign does not invalidate the document.

**Generated steps that were never performed.** A model will fill gaps with plausible actions. Every step must trace back to something you actually did in the recording.

**No version control.** Two copies in two folders means someone follows the old one. One document, one link.

**Writing for the writer.** Long preambles about the purpose of the process are for the author. Start with the first action.

## The judgment call

Document the process that costs you the most interruptions, not the one that is easiest to describe. One accurate SOP with its exceptions, tested on a new person, is worth more than twenty tidy documents nobody opens. If you cannot record yourself doing it, you do not know it well enough to document it yet.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the fastest way to write an SOP?

Record yourself doing the task while narrating, transcribe it, and have AI split the transcript into steps, decision points and exceptions. Ten minutes of narration beats an hour of typing.

### Why do SOPs go unused?

Usually because they live in a folder rather than in the tool where the work happens, and because they describe only the happy path. Put the steps in the task template and include the exceptions.

### How often should an SOP be reviewed?

Give every document an owner and a last-reviewed date, and put the next review in the calendar at publication. Six months without review usually means at least one wrong step.
