The most dangerous sentence in analytics is "the query ran." A generated SQL statement that executes without error and returns a plausible number is worse than one that fails, because nobody investigates a number that looks right. That is the lens to apply to every AI tool in this category: does it help you check the answer, or does it only help you produce one faster.

Prices were read on vendor pricing pages on November 27, 2026. Warehouse and compute costs are excluded, and they usually dwarf the software subscription.

## The short answer

| Scenario | Pick | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Team already on Microsoft | Power BI | Cheap per-user, familiar to stakeholders |
| Ad hoc questions over a warehouse | Hex or a model plus SQL | Notebook workflow with review |
| Lightweight datasets and ops tracking | Airtable | Relational views without a warehouse |
| Explaining a result to a non-analyst | Claude or ChatGPT | Rewrites the finding in plain language |
| Building dashboards fast | Power BI or Tableau | Both are faster with a model drafting measures |

## Where AI genuinely helps an analyst

Four places, in order of value.

It writes the first draft of a query, which you then check. It explains someone else's query, which is useful when you inherit a model you did not build. It documents: column descriptions, metric definitions, the README nobody wrote. And it translates a result into a sentence a stakeholder can repeat in a meeting, which is often the actual deliverable.

It does not know your data. It does not know that the orders table has duplicate rows from a failed sync in March, or that the "active user" definition changed in Q2. Those are the facts that decide whether a number is right.

## Power BI: the cheapest serious option

Power BI lists Pro at $14 per user a month paid yearly, and Premium Per User at $24 per user a month. For an organization already on Microsoft 365, that is the lowest-friction way to get governed dashboards in front of people who will actually open them.

The trap is capacity and licensing complexity. Pro is fine for internal sharing inside a small team. Once you need to share with external users or host large models, the pricing conversation changes, and the Copilot features sit behind additional licensing that is easy to underestimate.

## Airtable: the analyst's spreadsheet replacement

Airtable's free plan covers small datasets, with Team at $20 per user a month billed annually and Business at $45.

For analysts, the value is the middle ground between a spreadsheet and a warehouse: linked records, views, and a structure that survives being handed to an operations team. When the request is "track these 400 accounts and show me which are stale," Airtable is faster than a pipeline.

It is not a warehouse. Row limits and performance make it the wrong place for anything large, and migrating out later is real work.

## Hex and the notebook problem

Notebook tools have become the default for teams that want SQL, Python, and charts in one reviewable document. Hex's published pricing is compute-based, with query sizes priced from $0.32 for a large query up to $4.06 for a GPU-backed one, which means your bill tracks usage rather than seats.

The advantage over a chat window is auditability: the query, the output, and the narrative sit in one artifact a colleague can re-run. That is the property to demand from any AI analytics tool. If you cannot re-run it and get the same number, it is a demo, not a workflow.

## Claude and ChatGPT: the drafting and explaining layer

Both are good at the two ends of the job: writing a query from a schema you paste in, and explaining what a result means to someone who does not read charts.

The failure mode that causes real damage is silent join duplication. A model will write a join that multiplies rows and returns a total that is inflated by a plausible amount. Nothing errors. The number is just wrong.

The defence is mechanical: check row counts before and after each join, sanity-check a total against a known source, and never publish a number you have not reconciled once by hand.

## Where analytics teams get this wrong

The first mistake is trusting generated SQL without a row-count check. The second is letting a model define a metric. Definitions are governance, not generation.

The third is the dashboard nobody trusts. If a stakeholder finds one wrong number, they stop using the whole dashboard, and rebuilding that trust takes months. That is the real cost of an unreviewed AI-generated measure.

## Who should skip this list

Skip the AI analytics layer if your data quality is poor, because it will accelerate wrong answers. Skip Airtable if your datasets are large or your joins are complex. And skip any tool that will not show you the SQL it ran, which is most of the chat-style analytics products.

## What to buy first

Power BI if you are on Microsoft, Airtable if the work is operational, and one model subscription for query drafting and explanation. Then invest in the unglamorous thing that makes all of it work: a documented metric layer, so that the model and the human are computing the same definition.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does Power BI cost per user?

Power BI lists Pro at $14 per user a month paid yearly, with Premium Per User at $24. Copilot features may require additional licensing, which is easy to miss when budgeting.

### Can AI write reliable SQL?

It writes a good first draft, but it also produces joins that silently duplicate rows and inflate totals without erroring. Always compare row counts before and after a join and reconcile one total by hand.

### What is the biggest risk of AI analytics tools?

Losing trust in the dashboard. One wrong number that a stakeholder catches can end their use of the whole report, and rebuilding that confidence takes months.
