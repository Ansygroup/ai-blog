An indie studio is usually two to five people doing the work of twenty. That is why AI coding tools landed harder in game development than almost anywhere else: a solo developer with a good assistant can ship systems that used to require a programmer they could not afford. The catch is that a shipped game carries obligations, from engine licenses to storefront disclosure rules, and generated assets make those obligations harder to prove.

Prices were read on vendor pricing pages on November 27, 2026. Credit-based pricing in this category means your real cost depends on how much you lean on the tool, so the tiers below are starting points.

## The short answer

| Scenario | Pick | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |

| Editor work and small scripts | GitHub Copilot | Cheap entry, works inside the IDE |

## The two costs nobody budgets for

The first is context. A game codebase is large, and an assistant that only sees the open file will happily write a system that conflicts with one written last week. Tools that index the repository do better, and that difference shows up around month three.

The second is provenance. If a storefront or a publisher asks which assets were generated, you need an answer. Studios that keep a simple log, one line per generated asset with the tool, the date, and the prompt, have a much easier conversation than studios trying to reconstruct it later.

## Cursor: the codebase-aware editor

Cursor lists Free at $0, Plus at $8 a month, Premium at $30, Super at $100, Ultra at $200, and Teams at $30 or more per user a month.

For game work, the tier that matters is the one where the assistant can see enough context to be useful across files. That is usually Premium and above once a project has real size. The failure mode is subtle: an assistant that writes plausible code against an API that does not exist in your engine version. Compile early and often, and never accept a large diff you have not read.

## GitHub Copilot: the cheap second pair of eyes

Copilot lists Free at $0, Pro at $10 a month, Pro+ at $39, and Max at $100. Usage runs on GitHub AI Credits at $0.01 each, with a monthly allowance per plan and optional budget caps for extra usage.

For a solo developer, Pro is the sensible entry: autocomplete, inline chat, and enough agent usage to test whether the workflow suits you. The credit model is the thing to watch, because agent mode and code review consume credits faster than plain completion.

## Claude: the design bible

The most underrated use of a long-context model in game development is not code. It is holding the design document, the lore, and the dialogue style guide in one conversation so that a new system or a new line of dialogue does not contradict something written six months ago.

Claude's Pro plan is listed at $17 a month on annual billing or $20 monthly, with a 200,000-token context window that fits a surprising amount of design material. The failure mode is confident invention: a model will produce lore that sounds canonical and is not. Keep the canon in your own document and paste it in.

## Concept art: Midjourney and its limits

Image models are good at the first week of a project, when you need to find a visual direction fast. They are bad at consistency, which is what production needs: the same character, the same palette, the same silhouette across forty assets.

Use them to explore, then commission or build the assets you ship. And keep the generated images out of the build unless the tool's license clearly permits commercial use in a game, because the terms differ sharply between consumer and commercial tiers.

## Voice and audio: useful, with paperwork

Voice generation is now good enough for placeholder dialogue and, in some genres, for shipping. The constraint is consent and disclosure. Cloning a voice actor without a contract that covers synthesis is a lawsuit, and several storefronts now require disclosure of AI-generated content.

For music and sound effects, the same provenance rule applies. Log what you generate.

## Where indie teams get this wrong

The first mistake is trusting generated code in the systems that are hardest to debug: save/load, networking, and economy. Those are where a subtle bug costs a month.

The second is skipping the license read. Engine terms, asset store terms, and model terms stack, and the strictest one wins.

The third is underestimating review time. AI produces code faster than a small team can review it, and unreviewed code in a shipped game is technical debt with a store page.

## Who should skip this list

Skip the expensive tiers until you have a project that justifies them. Skip generative art entirely if your selling point is a hand-made look, because players notice. And skip any tool whose license you cannot explain to a publisher in one sentence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does Cursor cost for game development?

Cursor lists Free at $0, Plus at $8 a month, Premium at $30, Super at $100, Ultra at $200, and Teams from $30 per user. Premium and above is where codebase-wide context becomes useful on a real project.

### Can I ship AI-generated art in a commercial game?

Only if the tool's license permits commercial use and the storefront's disclosure rules are met. Licenses differ sharply between consumer and commercial tiers, so log each asset with its tool, date and prompt.

### What should an indie studio not use AI for?

Save systems, networking and economy code, which are the hardest to debug and the most expensive to get wrong. Also avoid generated assets in a game whose selling point is a hand-made art style.
