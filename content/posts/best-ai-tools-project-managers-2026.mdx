Every project tool now has an AI assistant, and almost every one of them is metered. That single fact changes the buying decision more than any feature comparison: a tool that charges per AI request can cost more in a busy quarter than the seats did all year. Project managers are the people who discover this in month three, when the invoice arrives and the budget line was set in January.

Prices were read on vendor pricing pages on November 27, 2026. Metered AI pricing is where you should spend your diligence, not on the feature grid.

## The short answer

| Scenario | Pick | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cross-functional projects with many stakeholders | Asana | Clear ownership model, but watch AI request costs |
| Operational boards and client work | Monday | Flexible boards, per-seat pricing |
| Engineering-heavy roadmaps | Linear | Fast, opinionated, cheap per seat |
| Documentation and project memory | Notion | The wiki that survives the project |
| Meeting notes and action items | Otter | Transcribes and extracts tasks |

## The AI request meter is the new seat

Asana lists Starter at $10.99 per user a month and Advanced at $24.99, with AI requests priced at $0.50 each when bought as prepaid packs or $0.60 each on pay-as-you-go billing.

Do the arithmetic before you roll it out. A team of ten running twenty AI-assisted actions a day, five days a week, is roughly 4,000 requests a month. At $0.50 that is $2,000, which is more than the seats. Most teams will not use it that heavily, and some will use it far more. The point is that "we will turn on AI later" is a budget decision, not a toggle.

The same pattern appears elsewhere: ClickUp meters agent usage in credits at $0.001 each, which is cheap per unit and easy to burn through once an agent runs on a schedule.

## Asana: structure for people who do not like structure

Asana's strength is accountability. Every task has one owner, every project has a status, and the update flow forces a weekly rhythm that most teams need. For a project manager coordinating people across departments, that is the product.

Where it struggles is flexibility. Teams that want a board shaped like their actual process end up fighting the tool. Teams that adopt Asana's model tend to keep it.

## Monday: boards people actually update

Monday's pricing is listed per seat, with a common tier at $28 per seat a month and example totals in the $36 to $93 a month range depending on seats and plan.

The reason it wins in operations-heavy teams is that a board is an easy thing to explain. A warehouse manager, a marketing lead, and a finance person can all read the same board without training. That adoption advantage is worth more than a feature list.

The trap is seat creep. Every stakeholder who wants to look at a board is a paid seat, and guest access is more limited than people expect.

## Linear: the engineering roadmap

Linear lists a free tier, $10 per user a month, and $16 per user a month for the higher plan. It is opinionated: cycles, issues, and a fast interface that engineers do not complain about.

For a project manager working with a product team, Linear is often the tool the engineers already use, which means the roadmap lives where the work happens. For everyone else, it is the wrong shape.

## Notion: the project's memory

Notion's paid plans are $10 per seat a month for Plus and $20 for Business, with a free tier that covers small teams.

The value in project work is not task tracking, it is the record: the decision log, the scope document, the retro notes. Projects fail from forgotten decisions more often than from missed tasks, and a searchable record is the cheapest insurance available.

## Otter: the meeting layer

Otter's plans are listed at $16.99 a month monthly or $8.49 a month on annual billing for the individual tier, with higher tiers at $30 and $24 and a $19.99 option between them. Student and teacher pricing is heavily discounted, at $6.67 a month billed annually.

For a project manager, the value is action items. A transcript that produces a task list you then paste into the project tool removes the twenty minutes after every meeting spent reconstructing what was agreed.

The failure mode is accuracy on names and numbers. Check the task list against your own notes before it becomes the record.

## Where project teams get this wrong

The first mistake is tool sprawl. A team running a project tool, a wiki, a separate meeting tool, and a spreadsheet is paying four subscriptions to store the same information badly.

The second is AI-generated status reports nobody reads. If the weekly update is written by a model from task titles, it will be accurate and useless. The useful part of a status update is the judgment about what is at risk.

The third is metered AI turned on for everyone by default. Set a budget, watch the first month, then decide.

## Who should skip this list

Skip the AI features on any of these tools until the underlying process is written down, because automation amplifies a bad process. Skip Linear unless your team is engineering-led. And skip the enterprise tiers entirely for teams under twenty people, where the admin features cost more than they save.

## What to buy first

One project tool that matches how your team already works, Notion for the record, and Otter for meeting notes. Then add AI features one at a time, with a budget cap, and measure whether the weekly update got shorter.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does Asana's AI cost?

Asana lists Starter at $10.99 and Advanced at $24.99 per user a month, with AI requests at $0.50 each in prepaid packs or $0.60 pay-as-you-go. Heavy use can exceed the seat cost.

### Is Monday or Asana better for a small team?

Monday suits operations teams that need boards anyone can read, with tiers around $28 per seat a month. Asana suits cross-functional projects that need clear single ownership and weekly status discipline.

### Do project managers need a separate meeting transcription tool?

Often yes. Otter is listed at $16.99 a month or $8.49 on annual billing, and the value is the extracted action items you paste into the project tool, not the transcript itself.
