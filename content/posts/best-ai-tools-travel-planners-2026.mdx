A travel planner sells certainty. The client pays so that the connection in Zurich works, the hotel has the room they asked for, and nobody spends the first day of the holiday on the phone. AI is very good at the itinerary draft and very bad at certainty, which is the whole reason this category needs a careful stack rather than an enthusiastic one.

Prices were read on vendor pricing pages on November 27, 2026. Airline and hotel prices change hourly, so nothing in this article quotes a fare.

## What AI can and cannot do in travel

It can build a plausible five-day itinerary in a city it has never been to, in about four seconds. It can suggest neighborhoods, walking routes, and restaurants that fit a budget. It can draft the pre-trip email that answers the twelve questions every client asks.

It cannot know that a museum closed for renovation in October, that a train line is replaced by buses on Sundays, or that the hotel's "sea view" faces a car park. Those are the details a planner is paid for, and every one of them has to be verified against a live source.

## TripIt Pro: the document layer

TripIt Pro is listed at $49 a year. You forward confirmations and it builds a master itinerary with flight alerts and alternate flight options.

For a planner managing several clients, the value is that the itinerary exists in one place the client can open on their phone, in the right time zone, with the confirmation numbers attached. The failure mode is that it is only as good as what you forward: a missing transfer shows up as a gap in the timeline, and the client discovers it at the airport.

## ChatGPT and Claude: the drafting engine

The best use is not the itinerary. It is the twenty emails around the itinerary: the pre-departure brief, the packing list adapted to the weather, the restaurant shortlist by neighborhood, the "what to do if your flight is cancelled" note.

Both are also good at the reasoning a client cannot do: "we land at 6am, the hotel check-in is 3pm, what do we do with two suitcases and a tired child." That is a planning problem with a real answer, and models handle it well.

The failure that damages trust is an invented specific. A flight number, a hotel policy, a visa rule, a train time. If it is in a client document, it came from a confirmation or an official page, not from a draft.

## Perplexity: the verification tool

Perplexity is the closest thing to a research assistant for the live details: current opening hours, whether a site requires advance booking, what a city's transit pass actually covers. It answers with links, and the links are the point.

The discipline is to open them. A cited page that does not say what the answer claims is the most common failure, and in travel it is the difference between a client standing outside a closed museum and a client having a good day.

## Calendly: the time zone arithmetic

Calendly's paid plans are listed at $10 and $16 a month. For a planner working across time zones, the value is that the client books in their own time and the meeting lands correctly in yours.

The mistake is leaving the booking page open-ended. Cap the hours you offer, or you will be taking calls at midnight because the page allowed it.

## Canva: proposals that look like a business

A travel proposal is a sales document. Canva turns a hotel list and a rough plan into something that looks considered, and the free tier is enough for most independent planners.

The AI features are not the reason to use it. The reason is that a client deciding between two planners will judge the document.

## Where travel AI goes wrong

The first problem is stale facts. Models have a training cutoff, and travel is the domain where that matters most: entry rules, transit schedules, and prices all move.

The second is fabricated availability. A model will happily confirm that a restaurant takes walk-ins. It does not know.

The third is client data. Passport numbers, dates of birth, and payment details do not belong in a chat window, and any tool that touches them needs to be one you would defend in a data protection conversation.

## Who should skip this list

Skip the AI itinerary tools if your value is personal knowledge of a destination, which no model replicates. Skip TripIt if your clients are not comfortable forwarding confirmations to a third party. And skip any automation that sends client documents without a human reading them first, because the one time it goes wrong, it goes wrong on someone's honeymoon.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can AI plan a full trip itinerary?

It can draft one quickly, but every specific detail needs verification against a live source. Opening hours, transit changes, hotel policies and prices all move, and a model's training data does not know today's schedule.

### How much does TripIt Pro cost?

TripIt Pro is listed at $49 a year. It builds a master itinerary from forwarded confirmations and adds flight alerts and alternate flight options.

### What is the biggest risk of using AI in travel planning?

Fabricated specifics. A model will state a flight time, visa rule or hotel policy with confidence. If it appears in a client document, it must come from a confirmation or an official page.
