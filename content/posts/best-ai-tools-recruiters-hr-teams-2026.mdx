Recruiting software has spent five years adding AI to the top of the funnel while the bottom of the funnel, the part where a candidate waits eleven days for a rejection, has barely changed. The tools below are judged on whether they shorten that wait and whether the bill survives a busy quarter. A pipeline that fills itself is useless if the per-seat and per-message costs double when hiring actually starts.

Prices were read on vendor pricing pages on November 27, 2026. Enterprise plans in this category are quoted, not listed, so treat any figure below as the starting point of a conversation.

## The short answer

## Where AI in recruiting actually breaks

Two failure modes dominate, and both are legal rather than technical. The first is a model that screens on proxies it was never asked to consider, such as graduation year or postcode, because those fields correlate with outcomes in the training data. The second is a model that summarizes an interview and quietly drops the candidate's own words, which is exactly the evidence you need if a decision is challenged.

Neither is solved by buying a tool with AI in the name. It is solved by keeping the human decision and the raw notes, and using the model for the boring middle: scheduling, reminders, and turning a 40-minute conversation into a structured summary you then check.

## Ashby: the one with a published price list

Ashby publishes its feature grid, which is rarer than it should be in this category. Two line items are worth knowing before you compare quotes: candidate texting runs $90 per 1,000 additional messages, and SSO costs $100 a month as an add-on.

That SMS price is the trap. A high-volume hiring push with automated text reminders can add more to the bill than the seats do. Model your message volume at peak, not at average.

Where Ashby earns its place: one system for pipeline, scheduling, and reporting means a candidate does not fall between two tools. Where it struggles: very large organizations with bespoke approval chains.

## Greenhouse: interview structure as the product

Greenhouse does not publish prices, which tells you most of what you need to know about the sales process. What it sells is structure: interview kits, scorecards, and a defined loop that makes it hard to hire on vibes.

If your problem is inconsistent interviews across twenty hiring managers, that structure is worth the quote. If your problem is that nobody replies to candidates, Greenhouse will not fix it.

## Meeting tools: Fireflies and Otter

Both record, transcribe, and summarize interviews and intake calls. The practical value is not the summary, it is the searchable transcript: when a hiring manager says a candidate mentioned a specific constraint, you can find the sentence.

The risk is consent and retention. Interview recordings are personal data with rules that vary by country, and a transcript tool that keeps everything forever is a liability. Set retention deliberately, and tell candidates what is recorded.

## Calendly: still the least glamorous useful tool

Calendly's paid plans are listed at $10 and $16 a month, with enterprise pricing starting far higher. It does one thing: it removes the four-email dance of finding a slot. For recruiters scheduling twenty interviews a week, that is several hours returned.

The failure mode is the candidate experience. A booking page that shows forty open slots reads as desperation. Cap availability and add a buffer between interviews.

## ChatGPT and Claude: drafting with a bias pass

The best use is the least exciting: turning a hiring manager's bullet points into a job description that does not exclude half the applicant pool, and drafting outreach that mentions something specific about the person.

The bias pass matters. Ask the model to flag gendered or age-coded language in a draft, then fix it yourself. Do not ask it to rank candidates. Every vendor now offers some version of that feature, and every version carries the same risk of encoding a proxy you did not intend.

## What the tools cannot do

They cannot make a hiring manager reply to a candidate. They cannot shorten a two-week approval chain. And they cannot fix a job description that describes a unicorn, which is the actual reason most roles stay open.

The measurable wins are narrow: faster scheduling, searchable notes, and fewer dropped candidates. Budget accordingly.

## Who should skip this list

Skip the applicant tracking systems if you hire fewer than five people a year, where a shared inbox and a spreadsheet are honestly fine. Skip meeting transcription if your interviews are already documented by the panel. And skip any AI screening feature in a jurisdiction that requires explainability, unless the vendor will show you how the model reaches a decision.

## What to buy first

Start with scheduling and notes, because those save hours immediately and carry little risk. Add an applicant tracking system when your pipeline exceeds what a spreadsheet can hold, and pick the one whose pricing you can actually read. Then use a general model for drafting, with a human pass on every job description and every rejection.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Ashby or Greenhouse better for a small recruiting team?

Ashby publishes its pricing and bundles pipeline, scheduling and reporting into one system, which suits teams of two to ten. Greenhouse is stronger when the problem is inconsistent interview structure across many hiring managers, but its pricing is quote-only.

### Should recruiters use AI to screen resumes?

With caution. Models can pick up proxies such as graduation year or location, and some jurisdictions require you to explain automated decisions. Keep the decision with a human and use AI for summaries and scheduling.

### What hidden costs appear in recruiting software?

Messaging and add-ons. Ashby lists candidate texting at $90 per 1,000 messages and SSO at $100 a month, both of which can exceed seat costs during a hiring push.
