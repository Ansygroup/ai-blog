Customer success runs on signals: a login that stops, a support ticket that repeats, a champion who changes job. The job is noticing early and saying something useful. AI tools have made the noticing cheaper and the saying something useful harder, because automated outreach has trained customers to ignore anything that reads like a sequence.

Prices were read on vendor pricing pages on November 27, 2026. Success platforms mostly quote privately, so the figures below come from the support and CRM layer.

## The short answer

| Scenario | Pick | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Support and success in one inbox | Intercom | Outcome pricing lines up with value |
| Small team without a success platform | HubSpot | Free CRM, cheap seats |
| Health signals from product and billing data | Your warehouse plus a model | Cheaper than a platform at small scale |
| Renewal and QBR documents | Notion or Gamma | Fast drafts, editable output |
| Call and QBR notes | Otter or Fireflies | Searchable record of what was promised |

## The economics of success software

Two pricing models dominate. Intercom charges per seat plus per outcome: seats are listed from $19 to $132 per seat a month depending on tier, and resolutions are priced at $0.99 per outcome. HubSpot's CRM is free, with Starter at $7 per seat a month and the next tier at $20.

Outcome pricing is honest in principle: you pay when the product resolves something. The risk is that it makes the cost of success variable in exactly the quarter when churn is high and volume spikes. Model a bad month, not a good one.

## Intercom: support and success in one thread

Intercom's advantage is that the conversation history and the customer record live together. A success manager can see the last four support tickets before a QBR, which is where most of the useful context actually is.

The AI features are strongest on deflection, which is a support metric, not a success metric. A resolved ticket is not a retained customer. Treat the automation as a way to free time for the conversations that prevent churn, not as a replacement for them.

## HubSpot: the cheap foundation

For a company under fifty people, HubSpot's free CRM plus Starter seats at $7 a month is enough to track accounts, log touchpoints, and build a renewal pipeline. It is not a success platform, and pretending otherwise leads to spreadsheets.

The value is that it is the system sales already uses, so success and sales argue from the same data rather than two exports.

## Health scores: the part AI makes worse

Every platform sells an AI health score. Almost every one is built on the data you have, which is usually incomplete: product usage if you instrumented it, billing if it is clean, support volume if the tags are consistent.

A model scoring accounts on bad data produces confident rankings that send your team to the wrong customers. The fix is boring: define five signals you trust, weight them yourself, and use AI to summarize the reason an account looks at risk, not to decide that it is.

## Claude and ChatGPT: the writing layer

The genuinely useful applications are narrow and valuable. Turning a call transcript into a QBR narrative with the customer's own words. Drafting the renewal email that references three specific outcomes. Rewriting an internal escalation so it is factual rather than emotional.

The failure mode is the generic check-in. A model asked to write a re-engagement email produces something cheerful and empty, and customers can spot it. If the message does not contain a specific fact about that account, do not send it.

## The QBR is where the hours go

A quarterly business review takes a success manager four to six hours to prepare, and most of that time is assembly rather than thinking: pulling usage numbers, pasting ticket themes, writing the same slide structure again. That is the part worth automating.

Build the deck template once, keep the numbers in a document you update monthly, and use a model to draft the narrative from the numbers you paste in. The judgment about what to recommend stays with you, which is correct, because that is the part the customer is paying for.

## Where success teams get this wrong

The first mistake is automating outreach before segmenting accounts. A generic sequence sent to your ten most important customers does more damage than sending nothing.

The second is trusting the health score without reading the inputs. The third is measuring success on activity, such as emails sent or QBRs held, rather than on renewal and expansion.

The fourth is silence. The most common reason customers churn is that nobody noticed something was wrong. A simple weekly review of accounts with falling usage beats any AI feature, and it takes twenty minutes.

## Who should skip this list

Skip a dedicated success platform until you have more than a hundred accounts or a team of three. Skip outcome-priced support tools if your volume is volatile, unless you can cap the spend. And skip AI outreach entirely for accounts in their first ninety days, where a human message is the whole point.

## What to buy first

A CRM everyone updates, one transcription tool for calls, and a model subscription for the writing. Then build the weekly at-risk review by hand before you automate it. The order matters: automation of a process nobody follows produces noise at scale.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How does Intercom's outcome pricing work?

Intercom charges seat fees listed from $19 to $132 per seat a month plus $0.99 per resolved outcome. It is honest in principle but makes spend spike in a bad quarter, so model a high-volume month.

### Are AI health scores reliable?

Only as reliable as the data behind them. Most companies have incomplete usage, billing or tagging data, so define five signals you trust and use AI to explain risk rather than to rank accounts.

### What is the biggest churn risk AI can help with?

Silence. A weekly human review of accounts with falling usage prevents more churn than any automated sequence, and AI helps most by summarizing why an account looks at risk.
