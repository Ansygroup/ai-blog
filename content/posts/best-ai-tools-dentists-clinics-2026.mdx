A dental practice loses more revenue to the front desk than to the chair. Empty slots from no-shows, unverified insurance discovered at checkout, recall lists that never get called. That is where AI tools earn their fee in a clinic, and it is also where they do the most damage when nobody checks the output.

Prices were read on vendor pricing pages on November 27, 2026. Practice management systems usually quote privately, so most of the numbers below come from scheduling, note-taking, and marketing tools rather than from the big clinical suites.

## Why the front desk is the first place to look

Three tasks consume the day: confirming appointments, checking insurance, and chasing recall. Each has a measurable failure cost. A no-show at a hygiene appointment is a slot you cannot refill. A rejected claim found three weeks later is staff time spent appealing instead of treating.

Software can help with all three, but only if the practice has a written policy for what happens when the automation is wrong. That is the part vendors skip in the demo.

## Jane: scheduling, charting, and a published price

Jane lists its plans at $59, $79, and $99 a month, with add-ons priced separately. Two of those add-ons matter for a clinic. AI Scribe runs $15 per practitioner a month for unlimited notes, with five free notes a month included on the base plans. Insurance billing adds $20 a month plus $5 per license.

Payment processing is where the real numbers hide: online card payments are listed at 2.85% plus 25 cents, and in-person card payments at 2.6% plus 10 cents. On a practice taking $80,000 a month by card, the difference between those two rates is a few hundred dollars a month, which is why moving routine payments to the terminal matters more than most software decisions.

Jane's weakness is specialization. A practice with complex periodontal charting or orthodontic treatment planning will find the clinical depth thinner than a dedicated dental suite. It fits a small general or multi-disciplinary clinic well.

## AI scribes: the highest-value add-on, and the riskiest

A scribe listens to the appointment and drafts the note. For a dentist writing fifteen notes at the end of the day, that is an hour recovered daily. Jane's $15 per practitioner price is representative of the category.

The risk is a note that says the patient reported something they did not, or omits the one finding that matters. Clinical notes are legal documents, and a model that sounds confident while inventing a detail is worse than a template. The workflow that holds up: the clinician reads every note before signing, and the practice spot-checks a sample monthly.

There is also a consent question. Patients should know when a recording is being made, and the practice should know where the audio is stored and for how long.

## Scheduling: Calendly and the reminder problem

Calendly's paid plans are listed at $10 and $16 a month. It is a reasonable bridge if your practice management system has no online booking, but it will not sync a chair schedule or check insurance eligibility.

The real value is reminders, and the numbers there are not about software. Reminder timing is a policy decision: two days out and one hour out, by SMS where the patient consented, with a clear cancellation window. Automating a bad reminder policy just annoys patients faster.

## ChatGPT and Claude: the plain-language layer

Models are genuinely good at two clinical-adjacent tasks: rewriting postoperative instructions at a sixth-grade reading level, and drafting the letter to an insurer that restates the clinical justification in the format the payer expects.

Neither should touch a diagnosis. And every patient-facing document needs a clinician's read before it goes out, because a plausible-sounding instruction that contradicts your actual protocol is a liability with your practice's name on it.

## Canva: marketing that stays local

For a single practice, marketing is mostly the things patients see in the waiting room and on their phone: service pages, offers, before-and-after layouts. Canva covers that at a cost that does not need a line in the budget.

The compliance issue is before-and-after imagery, which has its own rules in most jurisdictions. Check what your regulator allows before posting, and keep patient consent on file.

## Where clinics get this wrong

Three failures, in order of how often they happen.

Automating insurance verification without a human check on unusual plans. The claim that gets denied is almost always the one the automation approved.

Letting the scribe write the note and signing without reading. This is the single biggest liability in the category.

Buying a patient communication platform before fixing the recall list. If the list has wrong numbers and dead patients, a new tool just sends more messages into the void.

## Who should skip this list

Skip the AI scribe if your notes are already fast and your payers audit them closely. Skip a new practice management system mid-year, because migrating a chart mid-treatment is a risk you do not need. And skip patient-facing AI chat on your website until you have someone who can review transcripts weekly, because a chatbot giving clinical advice is a regulatory problem, not a support ticket.

## What to buy first

Fix the front desk before the chair. A scheduler with online booking, a reminder policy written down, and one AI scribe with a mandatory review step. Add marketing tools last, when the schedule is full and the problem is filling the quiet weeks instead.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does an AI scribe cost for a dental practice?

Jane lists unlimited AI Scribe at $15 per practitioner per month, with five free notes a month on the base plans. Dedicated scribe tools vary, but the per-clinician range is broadly similar.

### Can AI write clinical notes safely?

Only with a mandatory human review. A scribe can draft the note, but the clinician must read and correct it before signing, because an invented or missing finding in a clinical record is a legal problem.
