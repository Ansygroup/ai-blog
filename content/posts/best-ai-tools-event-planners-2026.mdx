An event planner's reputation is made in the last forty minutes before doors open, when a speaker is missing, the caterer is late, and the slide deck is on the wrong laptop. No AI tool fixes that. What AI tools can do is remove the eighty hours of preparation that make those forty minutes survivable.

Prices were read on vendor pricing pages on November 27, 2026. Ticketing fees change more often than software subscriptions, so check the current rate card before you price a client's budget.

## The short answer

| Scenario | Pick | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paid public events with a box office | Eventbrite | Built-in discovery, but read the per-ticket fee |
| Private corporate events and retainer clients | HoneyBook | Proposals, contracts and invoices in one place |
| Run-of-show and vendor coordination | Notion | One shared document the whole crew can open |
| Decks and sponsor packages | Gamma | Fast structure, editable output |
| Guest communication | ChatGPT or Claude | Drafts the eleven emails you send every time |

## Where the money goes in event software

Two costs dominate and only one is visible. The subscription is easy to compare. The per-ticket fee is not, because it scales with the event rather than the month.

Eventbrite's fee structure is listed at 3.7% plus $1.79 per paid ticket, with Eventbrite Pro starting at $15 a month for organizers who need the marketing tools and higher email limits. On a 500-ticket event at $80 a ticket, that per-ticket fee adds up to roughly $2,375 across the run. That number belongs in the client's budget, not in your margin.

For free events, the calculation is different and often much kinder, which is why community events and paid conferences should not be planned on the same platform by default.

## Eventbrite: reach versus cost

The reason to use Eventbrite is discovery. Attendees search it, and a listing on a platform people already browse converts better than a page nobody visits. For consumer events where strangers need to find you, that reach is worth the fee.

The reason to avoid it is control. You do not own the attendee relationship, the fees are visible to buyers, and the platform's own branding sits on your checkout. For a corporate client paying for a private event, that is the wrong impression.

## HoneyBook: the business side of planning

HoneyBook's plans are listed from $18 a month, with a free trial, and payment processing at 2.7% plus 10 cents for cards and 1.5% for bank transfers.

For planners, the value is the sequence: proposal, contract, deposit, final invoice, all in one thread the client can see. Events generate a lot of paperwork under time pressure, and the planner who sends a clean contract in ten minutes wins the next job.

The limitation is scale. A single operator with a dozen events a year fits well. A team with three coordinators and a pool of freelancers will hit the ceiling on shared inboxes.

## Notion: the run-of-show document

Nothing in this list saves more stress than one shared document that holds the timeline, the vendor contacts, the load-in windows, and the decisions. Notion's paid plans are listed at $10 per seat a month for Plus and $20 for Business, with a free tier that covers a small team.

The AI features are secondary. What matters is that the document can be opened by a venue manager at 6am and understood without a call. Build the run-of-show template once and clone it for every event.

## Gamma: decks without the design tax

Gamma's pitch is turning an outline into a presentation. For sponsor packages, speaker briefs, and the recap deck you send on Monday, that is a real saving, and the output is editable rather than a picture of slides.

The failure mode is sameness. Every Gamma deck has a family resemblance, and a sponsor paying five figures notices when the deck looks like a template. Use it for internal documents and the first draft of external ones, then rebuild the client-facing deck yourself.

## ChatGPT and Claude: the email engine

Event planning is an email job with a party attached. The same eleven messages go out for every event: save the date, the call for speakers, the vendor brief, the final details, the thank you. Models write all of them quickly, in your voice, if you give them two examples of emails you have already sent.

The failure that hurts is invented logistics. A model will happily write a load-in time that you never confirmed. Every specific time, address, and policy in an event email has to come from a document you checked.

## Where event AI goes wrong

The first problem is attendee data. Registration lists contain names, emails, dietary needs, and sometimes accessibility requirements. Before you paste any of that into a model, know where it is stored and who can read it.

The second is the run-of-show generated by a model that has never been to the venue. It will produce a plausible timeline that ignores the fact that the loading dock opens at 7am and the AV team needs ninety minutes.

The third is the confident answer about a vendor's terms. Cancellation policies, insurance requirements, and deposit schedules are contract facts. Read the contract.

## Who should skip this list

Skip paid ticketing platforms if your events are invitation-only and free, where a form and a spreadsheet do the job. Skip Gamma if your deck is the product. And skip AI drafting entirely if your client relationships are built on how you write, which for many planners is exactly the case.

## The stack I would run

Eventbrite only for public consumer events where discovery matters. HoneyBook for the contract and the invoice. Notion for the run-of-show, shared with the venue. Gamma for internal decks. One model subscription for the email treadmill. That covers a full season of events without a single enterprise contract.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does Eventbrite charge per ticket?

Eventbrite lists a service fee of 3.7% plus $1.79 per paid ticket, with Eventbrite Pro starting at $15 a month for extra marketing tools. On a 500-ticket event at $80, that is roughly $2,375 in fees.

### What is the most useful AI tool for an event planner?

Usually a shared run-of-show document rather than a generative tool. Notion at $10 per seat a month keeps the timeline, vendors and decisions in one place that a venue manager can open without a call.

### Can AI write the vendor and attendee emails for an event?

Yes, for drafts. Give the model two examples of emails you have sent before, then check every time, address and policy against the contract, because models invent logistics that sound correct.
