If you write English as a second language professionally, you already know the real problem is rarely grammar. It is idiom, register, and the hundred micro-choices that make a sentence sound natural instead of translated. The tools below attack those problems separately, and mixing them up is where most people waste money.

## Who this is for, and who should skip it

This is for professionals, students, and freelancers who write emails, reports, papers, or client deliverables in English as an additional language. Skip the paraphrasing tools entirely if your goal is academic or professional authorship: a paragraph you did not write, run through a spinner, is both a plagiarism risk and a voice killer, and it solves a problem you do not have.

## Correction and fluency: Grammarly and ProWritingAid

Grammarly is the default for a reason: it sits in your browser, email, and docs, catching agreement errors, article mistakes (the eternal "a/the" problem), and preposition slips as you type. The free tier covers basics; Pro starts at $12 a month billed annually as of November 2026 and adds tone suggestions, rewrites, and fluency fixes that target exactly the "correct but awkward" zone non-native writers know well.

ProWritingAid is the stronger pick for long documents: reports, theses, manuscripts. It runs deeper reports on sentence variety, repeated sentence starts, and readability, and its pricing as of November 2026 starts around $10 a month billed annually, with monthly and lifetime options. The tradeoff: it lives in its own editor more than in your browser, so it rewards a paste-and-review session rather than inline correction. Our [Grammarly alternatives for freelancers](/posts/best-ai-tools-like-grammarly-for-freelancers-2026) and [Grammarly alternatives for teachers](/posts/best-ai-tools-for-teachers-2026-grammarly-alternatives) roundups compare the wider field.

The failure mode both share: over-correction. These tools are trained toward one register, polite American business English, and they will steadily sand off anything unusual, including phrasing that was deliberately chosen. Accept the grammar fixes; reject the personality edits.

## Rewriting and register: Claude or ChatGPT

A chatbot is the right tool for the question grammar checkers cannot answer: "does this sound like how a native professional would say it?" Paste a draft email and ask for three versions at different registers (warmer, more direct, more formal) with the changes explained. The explanation is the learning; the rewrite is the deliverable. Claude Pro is $20 a month (about $17 annual) and ChatGPT Plus is $20 a month as of November 2026. Claude tends to preserve your voice while fixing idiom; ChatGPT is faster for quick variations.

Where this fails: asking "make this sound native" without constraints produces generic corporate paste. Give the model your context (who the reader is, what relationship you have, what you want them to do) and keep your own sentences where they were already fine. Our [Claude tools for translators](/posts/best-ai-claude-tools-translators-2026) guide covers voice-preserving prompting in depth.

## Translation-assisted drafting: DeepL

Many strong writers draft difficult passages in their first language and translate. DeepL is the best machine translator for this, with free and paid Pro tiers that raise monthly character and file-translation limits. The workflow that works: draft in your strongest language, translate with DeepL, then rewrite the English output in your own voice rather than shipping it raw. Raw machine translation has a tell: flawless grammar, slightly wrong word choices ("economic" when you meant "financial"), and idioms that land sideways.

## Build the feedback loop, not just the fix

The writers who improve fastest use these tools as teachers, not editors. When Grammarly or Claude rewrites one of your sentences, ask why. Keep a running doc of your recurring errors (article misuse, preposition choice, overlong sentences) and the corrected versions, and review it monthly. Within a few months you will see your own pattern: maybe you drop articles before abstract nouns, or you default to "very" plus an adjective where English has a single stronger word. That pattern list is worth more than any subscription, because it shrinks. If your correction count is not going down over time, the tool is editing your writing but not improving it, and that is a sign to slow down and study the fixes instead of clicking accept.

## A warning about AI detectors

If you are a student or job applicant, know this: a 2023 Stanford-led study published in Patterns found that GPT detectors were biased against non-native English writing, flagging human-written TOEFL essays as AI-generated far more often than native writing. The cruel irony is that the cleaner your grammar tools make your prose, the more "AI-like" a lazy detector thinks it is. Protect yourself: keep drafts and version history that show your writing process, and if a school or employer accuses you based on a detector score alone, that study is your starting citation.

## How to decide

1. Making small errors in daily email and docs? Grammarly Pro.
2. Writing long reports, theses, or manuscripts? ProWritingAid's deep reports.
3. Sentences correct but sounding translated? Claude Pro, with register-specific prompts and explanations turned on.
4. Thinking in another language for hard passages? DeepL for the bridge, then rewrite in your own voice.

The goal is not to sound like a native speaker. It is to sound like yourself with the friction removed, and every tool here works better when you treat its output as a suggestion to judge, not a correction to accept.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best AI grammar checker for non-native English?

Grammarly Pro (from $12/mo annually as of November 2026) for daily inline correction, ProWritingAid (from about $10/mo annually) for long documents. Accept grammar fixes; reject personality edits.

### Can AI make my English sound native?

It can fix idiom and register, especially Claude with register-specific prompts. But unconstrained 'make this sound native' prompts produce generic corporate prose. Keep your own sentences where they already work.

### Do AI detectors unfairly flag non-native writers?

Yes. A 2023 Stanford-led study in Patterns found GPT detectors biased against non-native English writing, misflagging human-written TOEFL essays as AI-generated far more often. Keep drafts and version history as evidence of your process.

### Is DeepL good for drafting in English?

Good as a bridge: draft in your strongest language, translate, then rewrite the output in your own voice. Raw machine translation has flawless grammar but slightly wrong word choices.
