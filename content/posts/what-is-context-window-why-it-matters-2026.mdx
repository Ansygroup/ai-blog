Every AI tool landing page in 2026 brags about context windows. One million tokens. Two million. Ten million. The numbers keep doubling, and they matter far less than the marketing suggests. Here is what a context window actually is, why bigger stopped meaning better, and how to choose tools based on it without getting fooled.

## The two-minute definition

A context window is the amount of text an AI model can consider at one time: your conversation history, the documents you pasted, the instructions you gave, and the answer it is currently writing, all together. It is measured in tokens, which are chunks of text. A token is roughly three-quarters of an English word, so a 200,000-token window holds about 150,000 words, or a 500-page novel.

Everything inside the window, the model can "see." Everything outside it might as well not exist. When a chatbot forgets what you said at the start of a long conversation, the window is usually why: the oldest messages scrolled out.

## Why the size wars are misleading

Three practical problems shrink the usable window far below the advertised number.

First, attention fades in the middle. Models are best at using information at the very start and very end of their context. Content buried in the middle of a huge paste gets retrieved less reliably. Researchers call this "lost in the middle," and while newer models handle it better, the effect has not disappeared at any window size. A model with a 200K window that reliably uses 200K beats a model with a 1M window that gets fuzzy past 300K.

Second, big contexts cost money and time. Every token in the window is processed on every single request. Paste a 300-page contract into each message of a fifty-message conversation and you are paying to re-read that contract fifty times, and waiting for it each time. This is why tools like NotebookLM use retrieval tricks instead of brute-force context: they fetch only the relevant pages per question, which is faster, cheaper, and often more accurate.

Third, output counts against the window. If the model is writing a long answer, that answer consumes window space too. On a small-window model, asking for a long summary of a long document can push the beginning of the document out of view mid-answer.

## What window size do you actually need

Match the window to the job, not the marketing:

- Chat and drafting: even 32K tokens is generous. Your messages plus a few pasted articles rarely exceed a few thousand tokens.
- Analyzing one long document (a book manuscript, a legal filing): 128K to 200K covers nearly everything, and retrieval-based tools handle the rest.
- Whole codebases or dozens of documents at once: this is the legitimate use case for 1M+ windows, and the main reason tools built on Gemini's large-context models exist.
- Agents and long-running tasks: the window matters less than how the tool manages memory, summarizing and re-injecting what matters.

## How to test a tool's real context handling

You do not need to take any vendor's word for it. A five-minute test tells you more than a spec sheet. Take a long document, paste it in, and hide a specific unusual fact near the middle: a name, a number, a made-up term like "the Fendelton clause." Then ask about that fact. Try the same thing near the beginning and near the end. Tools that answer all three correctly are using their window well. Tools that nail the beginning and end but miss the middle have the classic attention gap, no matter how large the advertised window is. Repeat the test at a few document lengths, because degradation is usually gradual, not a cliff.

A second test: ask the model to quote a sentence verbatim from a specific page. Quoting forces it to actually retrieve rather than answer from general knowledge, and you can check the quote against the source. Summaries can be faked from partial understanding; verbatim quotes cannot.

## A better question to ask

Instead of "how big is the context window," ask "how does this tool handle information that does not fit?" Good tools retrieve, summarize, and cite. Weak tools silently truncate, and you find out when the model confidently answers a question about page 12 using nothing from page 12. Our [Claude 4 vs GPT-5 comparison](/posts/claude-4-vs-gpt-5-honest-comparison-2026) touches on this in practice: window size on paper told us less than testing where each model started answering from memory instead of the document.

The context window is a spec worth knowing. It is just no longer the spec that decides which tool is better. The vendors figured out that buyers compare numbers, so they compete on the one number that is easiest to inflate. Your defense is simple: know what a window is, know roughly how many tokens your real work needs, and test retrieval quality yourself. Once you do, the marketing slides stop working on you, and the tools that quietly handle your documents well become easy to spot.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a context window in AI?

The amount of text a model can consider at once, measured in tokens. Your chat history, pasted documents, instructions, and the model's answer all share this space.

### How many words is 200K tokens?

Roughly 150,000 English words, about a 500-page novel. A token is around three-quarters of a word on average.

### Is a 1 million token context window worth paying for?

Only for specific jobs like whole-codebase analysis. For chat and single documents, 128K to 200K is plenty, and how a tool retrieves information matters more than raw window size.
