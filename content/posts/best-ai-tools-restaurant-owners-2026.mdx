A restaurant owner has about four spare minutes a day, and most software pitches ignore that. The AI tools worth paying for in a restaurant are the ones that remove a recurring chore without adding a screen to watch: the weekly schedule, the supplier email, the menu description, the reply to a one-star review. Everything else is a demo.

Prices below were read on vendor pricing pages on November 27, 2026. Last tested: November 27, 2026. Toast is the one restaurant-specific vendor with usable published prices; the rest are general tools.

## The short answer

| Scenario | Pick | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening a new restaurant and choosing a POS | Toast | Published entry prices, scheduling and guest tools in one system |
| Menu copy, specials and review replies | Claude Pro | Fast drafts in your voice, $17 annual or $20 monthly |
| Menu and social graphics | Canva | Templates for menus and posts; check its current plans before buying |
| Reservations and event bookings | Calendly | Private dining and tasting inquiries without phone calls, from $10 per seat |

## Toast: the POS that bundles the chores

Toast listed a Starter Kit starting at $0 per month, a Point of Sale plan starting at $69 per month, and a higher tier starting at $90 per month. A new-restaurant offer on the page read $69 per month plus $9 per employee. Every tier's footnote said fees may apply, and payment processing, hardware and add-ons are separate lines.

Guest CRM, insights and feedback, plus timekeeping and basic scheduling, were marked included in all four columns of the comparison table. For an owner, that matters more than any AI feature: the sales, labor and guest data live in one place, so any assistant you add later has something accurate to read.

Where it fails: the headline price is a floor. Ask for the full quote with processing rates, hardware, and the contract term in writing before signing. Compare it with a second vendor on the same sheet, line by line.

## Claude for menus, specials and reviews

Claude Pro was $17 per month billed annually or $20 monthly on the page read. In a restaurant it earns its price in three places.

Menu descriptions: give it the ingredients and the cooking method and ask for 20 words that describe the dish without adjectives like "mouthwatering." Specials: paste what is overstocked and ask for three dish ideas using the existing prep. Reviews: paste the review and the facts from your records, and ask for a reply that is short, specific and non-defensive.

Where it fails: allergens. Never let a draft describe a dish as "gluten free" or "nut free" on the strength of a chat tool. The kitchen's actual allergen process decides that, and a menu error here is a safety issue, not a style one.

## Canva for menus and posts

Canva's pricing page returned nothing readable when checked, so no figure is quoted. The free version is enough to build a printed menu, a table card and weekly Instagram posts from templates. The practical gain is consistency: one set of colors and fonts across menu, window sign and posts looks more established than three styles.

## Calendly for private dining and events

Calendly listed paid tiers at $10 and $16 per seat. Restaurants get repeated inquiries for private rooms and group bookings. A form that asks party size, date and budget before offering a call slot filters out time-wasters and gives the manager the facts at the start of the conversation.

## What the vendor pages do not say

The labor schedule is where an AI-style assistant pays back fastest, and it is the least glamorous. The weekly schedule for a 15-person team is a puzzle of availability, hours caps and expected covers. Tools that generate a draft from last year's sales by day of week save a manager an hour or two a week. The failure mode is trusting the forecast on a week with a local event, a holiday or a road closure that the data cannot see. Add a manual column for known events before you publish.

A second gap is the tipping and payroll side. Scheduling software cannot decide overtime rules for your state. Keep your payroll provider's rules as the final check, and treat any generated schedule as a draft.

## Who should skip this list

If your POS contract has two years left, switching for AI features is expensive. Use the general tools for writing and leave the POS alone until renewal. Food trucks and very small counters with one or two staff can skip scheduling tools entirely and use a shared calendar.

## What to buy first

Spend nothing on new software for two weeks. Instead, time the chores: scheduling, supplier orders, menu changes, reviews. Pick the one that costs the most hours. If it is writing, buy Claude Pro for one month. If it is scheduling and sales data, request a Toast quote and a competitor's on the same terms. A $20 test beats a 12-month contract signed on a demo.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Toast's $69 plan the real price?

It is the entry price for the Point of Sale plan on the page read. Processing, hardware and add-ons are separate, so ask for a full written quote.

### Can AI write allergen information for a menu?

No. Allergen claims must come from your kitchen's own process. Use AI for tone and layout only.

### What should a restaurant automate first?

The chore that costs the most hours. For many owners that is the weekly schedule or review replies, not a front-of-house gadget.
