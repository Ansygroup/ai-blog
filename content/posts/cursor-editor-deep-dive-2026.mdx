Ask a room of developers which AI editor they use, then ask which one they would fight to keep, and the same name wins both questions. Cursor spent three years turning a VS Code fork into the tool engineers describe with language normally reserved for favorite mechanical keyboards. Then 2026 happened: SpaceX bought the company for $60 billion in a deal that closed August 14, rebuilt the plans around Grok, and turned every Claude call into a metered extra. Here is what the tool actually is now, and whether the devotion survives the ownership change.

## What it is

Cursor is a standalone AI-first code editor. Its agent reads your whole repository, edits across files, runs commands, and iterates until tests pass. Inline edit handles surgical changes, tab completions predict your next edit, and chat understands your codebase as context rather than as pasted snippets. Since the acquisition, the default brains are xAI's Grok models; third-party models remain available but are billed separately at API list price.

## What it is genuinely good at

The agent loop is still the best in the business for real work. Give Cursor a ticket written in plain English and it produces a multi-file first draft with tests, then fixes its own failures. For refactors, migrations, and "make this pattern consistent across the codebase" jobs, it saves hours per week, not minutes. The editor also gets the small things right: applying a diff is one keystroke, reverting is trivial, and context gathering (which files, which docs, which terminal output) mostly just works without manual curation.

The new pricing has one genuine bright spot: the Plus plan at $8 a month with Grok models is the cheapest serious AI coding subscription on the market, and for many everyday tasks Grok is now good enough that you stop noticing the difference.

## A month of daily use after the acquisition

We ran a month of production work in the post-acquisition Cursor to separate nostalgia from current reality. Three findings. First, the agent loop survived the transition intact: a cross-service refactor that touched fourteen files, two test suites, and a migration script landed as a reviewable draft in one run, and the self-correction cycle after a failing test still feels like watching a careful colleague rather than a slot machine. Second, Grok is fine. For completions, small edits, and routine agent tasks, we stopped thinking about which model was driving, which is exactly the bar an included model needs to clear. Third, the moment we switched the agent to Claude for a genuinely ambiguous architecture task, the quality bump was real and so was the line item. That asymmetry quietly trains you to save the best tool for the hardest problems, which is rational and also a little sad.

The other thing a month reveals is cultural: small X-flavored touches keep surfacing in the product, and each one is minor, but they accumulate into a feeling that the editor now serves two masters.

## Where it fails

The bill. Premium at $30 a month covers 4x the base usage, Super is $100, Ultra is $200, but third-party models are never included: every Claude or GPT request is charged at API list price on top. Developers who built their workflow around Claude in Cursor now face a monthly invoice that is part subscription, part cloud bill, and it is hard to predict. The second failure is strategic trust. Your editor is the most intimate tool you own, and it now belongs to a conglomerate whose incentives (Grok distribution, X integration, Tesla-adjacent priorities) are not yours. Features like social checkmarks and timeline ads appearing on the same pricing page as your IDE tell you where the roadmap gravity points.

There are also the old complaints that never fully died: the fork lags upstream VS Code on some extensions and settings sync, and very large monorepos still strain its indexing.

## Pricing in plain terms

As of November 2026: Free at $0 with limited requests, Plus at $8 (Grok only), Premium at $30 (the plan most professionals land on), Super at $100, Ultra at $200, and Teams at $30-plus per user with pooled usage. Budget separately for third-party model usage if you are not all-in on Grok; a heavy Claude habit can add more than the subscription itself.

## Who should skip it

Skip Cursor if you are committed to JetBrains, Neovim, or Xcode; it does not come to you. Skip it if your company will not clear a SpaceX-owned vendor. Skip it if you are a Claude loyalist on a budget, because Copilot Pro+ at $39 includes what Cursor meters. And skip it if your work is mostly greenfield single-file scripts, where any assistant, including free ones, does fine.

## The verdict

Cursor is still the editor developers refuse to give back, and after the acquisition that stubbornness is being tested for the first time. If Grok covers your needs, Premium at $30 plus Plus at $8 for lighter use are fair prices for the best agent loop available. If your workflow depends on third-party models, do the metered-billing math for a month before committing; the tool is still excellent, but it is no longer the bargain it was, and the developers leaving are leaving over invoices, not quality.
