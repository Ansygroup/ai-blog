The trainers who keep clients past month three are not the ones with the best programs. They are the ones who answer messages on a Sunday and notice when someone stops logging sessions. That is the job AI can actually help with, and it is not the job most training software markets.

Prices below were read on vendor pricing pages on November 27, 2026. Coaching platforms price by client count and change tiers often, so verify against your own roster size.

## The short answer

| Scenario | Pick | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solo trainer, under 30 clients | Everfit | Per-client pricing that scales down as you grow |
| Programming and habit tracking for a small roster | TrueCoach | Clean workout delivery, low friction for clients |
| Studio with several coaches | Trainerize | Team accounts and add-on depth |
| Client check-in replies and program drafts | ChatGPT or Claude | Fast first drafts you still have to edit |
| Voice notes and form cues | ElevenLabs | Turns written cues into audio a client can replay |

## What coaching software is really selling

Every platform in this category sells the same three things: a place to put programs, a place for clients to log, and a reason for the client to open the app. The differences that matter in practice are how fast a client can log a set, whether the app nags them on your behalf, and what happens to your bill when you add ten clients.

That last one is where most trainers get surprised.

## Everfit: the per-client curve

Everfit lists its entry plan at $19 a month billed monthly or $16 a month billed annually, and prices by client count after that: $29 for 10 clients, $49 for 20, $75 for 35, $95 for 50, and $220 for 200. The per-client cost falls from about $3.80 at the smallest tier to roughly $1.10 at the largest.

That curve is the honest way to price coaching software, because it matches how a trainer's revenue grows. The trap is the jump points. If you are at 21 clients, you pay for the 35-client tier, so the twenty-second client costs more than the twenty-first. Plan your intake in blocks that fit the tier you are on.

## TrueCoach: the one clients actually open

TrueCoach's plans are listed at $26.34, $57.99, and $136.99 a month, with the price rising as you add clients. Its advantage is client-side simplicity: a workout shows up, the client taps through it, the notes come back to you. Trainers consistently report fewer "what was I supposed to do today" messages, which is the whole point.

Where it falls down is the business side. Payments, contracts, and nutrition tracking are thinner than the studio platforms, so most trainers pair it with a separate scheduler and invoicing tool.

## Trainerize: studio depth, add-on economics

Trainerize lists add-ons from $10 a month, a one-time $169 setup fee on its Pro tier, and an AI add-on at $149 plus $0.10 per AI action. That per-action charge is the detail to read twice. A studio that leans on automated check-in messages can generate thousands of actions a month, and the metered cost can exceed the base subscription.

For a single trainer, Trainerize is more platform than needed. For a gym with three coaches and a shared client roster, the team features justify the complexity.

## ChatGPT and Claude: program drafts and check-ins

This is the most useful place for AI in the job, and the most dangerous. A model can turn a client's intake notes into a four-week progression, rewrite it for a home gym, and draft the check-in message that asks about sleep without sounding like a form.

What it cannot do is know your client's shoulder. Program generation without a human pass on injury history, movement quality, and the exercises the client actually enjoys is how trainers lose clients and, in the worst case, get someone hurt. Treat output as a first draft written by an enthusiastic intern.

The second failure is tone. Check-in messages written by a model tend toward the cheerful and generic. A client paying $200 a month can tell.

## ElevenLabs: voice cues that stick

ElevenLabs lists plans from $6 a month for its entry tier, $22 for Creator, $99 for Pro, and $299 for Scale, with annual pricing below those. For trainers, the use case is narrow but real: record a set of form cues in your own voice so a client can replay the cue at the gym instead of texting you mid-session.

It is a retention tool disguised as a novelty. It is also easy to overbuy. The entry tier covers hundreds of short clips.

## Where the money leaks

Three places.

Per-client tiers that charge for inactive clients. Audit your roster monthly and archive anyone who has not trained in six weeks.

Metered AI add-ons, as above. If a platform charges per action, model the cost at your real message volume before you turn it on.

Payment processing on top of subscriptions. Most platforms take a cut of client payments, and for a trainer billing 40 clients, that cut is often larger than the software fee.

## Who should skip this list

Skip the platforms if you coach fewer than ten clients and train in person, where a shared spreadsheet and a messaging app are genuinely enough. Skip AI program generation if you cannot commit to reviewing every session you send. And skip any annual contract in your first year, because roster size moves too fast to lock in.

## What to actually buy

Start with the platform that matches your roster size, not the one with the best feature list. Everfit if you are growing and want cost to track clients. TrueCoach if client adherence is your problem. Trainerize if you have coaches to coordinate. Then add one model subscription for drafting, and keep the injury history and the judgment calls where they belong.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which coaching platform is cheapest for a new personal trainer?

Everfit is usually the lowest entry point, listed at $19 a month billed monthly or $16 billed annually for its smallest tier. TrueCoach starts around $26.34 a month and Trainerize is aimed at studios rather than solo trainers.

### Should personal trainers use AI to write workout programs?

For first drafts only. A model can build a sensible four-week progression from intake notes, but it cannot assess injury history or movement quality, so every session needs a human review before a client sees it.

### What is the hidden cost in training software?

Metered add-ons and client-count tiers. Trainerize lists an AI add-on at $149 plus $0.10 per AI action, and Everfit's tiers mean adding one client can push you into a higher bracket.
