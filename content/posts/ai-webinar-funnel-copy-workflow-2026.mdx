A webinar funnel has four pieces of copy and one of them decides the result. It is not the registration page, and it is not the replay email. It is the promise on the registration page, because everything downstream inherits it: the show-up rate, the questions people ask, and whether the offer at the end lands as a solution or a bait and switch.

AI is good at producing the other three pieces quickly. The promise still has to come from you, and the workflow below is built around protecting it.

*Last tested: November 2026.*

## Step 1: Write one promise and one objection

One sentence each, on paper, before any prompt:

- Promise: what the attendee can do after the hour that they cannot do now.
- Objection: the reason they think it will not work for them.

Everything in the funnel either supports the promise or answers the objection. Anything that does neither is decoration, and decoration is what makes a registration page read like every other registration page.

## Step 2: Build the registration page in five blocks

| Block | Job | Length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Headline | The promise, in the attendee's words | Under 12 words |
| Three bullets | What they will leave with | One line each |
| Who it is for | Specific role or situation | Two sentences |
| Who it is not for | The disqualifier | One sentence |
| Form | Date, time, timezone, one field | Minimum |

The "who it is not for" line does more work than anything else on the page. It raises show-up rates because the people who register feel chosen, and it lowers refunds because the wrong people self-select out.

Generate five headline variants and pick the plainest. Then rewrite it once by hand, because the model will produce something a marketer would write and a buyer would skim.

## Step 3: Give each reminder email a different job

Three emails, three jobs, no repetition:

1. 24 hours before: the agenda and the one thing to bring.
2. 1 hour before: the join link and the time in their timezone.
3. 10 minutes before: one line, the link, nothing else.

Do not use any of them to sell. The reminders exist to get people in the room; the selling happens live, where you can hear objections.

## Step 4: Script the open and the close, improvise the middle

The first five minutes and the last ten decide whether the hour converts. Write both.

The open states the promise, the agenda and the payoff for staying to the end. The close restates the promise, names the offer, gives the price plainly, and states exactly what happens next.

Everything between them can be a slide outline plus your own knowledge. Generated middles sound like a lecture; a live middle sounds like a person.

## Step 5: Write the replay email before the event

Write it in advance so it goes out within an hour of the end, while the recording is still warm. Three elements: the link, the three timestamps worth watching, and the deadline for the offer.

Be careful with deadlines. A deadline that resets every week trains your list to ignore all of them. If you set one, honour it.

## Step 6: Test two promises, not two headlines

The headline test is cheap and the promise test is the one that matters. Run the same event twice with two different promises, or run two registration pages against different traffic sources, and compare show-up rate and offer conversion rather than click-through.

That comparison tells you which promise your audience actually wants, which is the input for every future webinar.

## Step 7: Keep a swipe file of objections

Every question in the chat is copy. Save them, group them, and use them as the bullets on the next registration page and the FAQ in the follow-up. Two or three events in, you will have a better objection list than any competitor research.

## What can go wrong

**Promise inflation.** "Build a six-figure business" fills a room and empties a list. Promise what the hour delivers.

**Invented proof.** Never let a model write a testimonial, a statistic or a case study. One fabricated result is a reputational event, and readers can tell.

**Fake scarcity.** Countdown timers that reset and seats that never run out are the fastest way to lose a sophisticated audience.

**Too many calls to action.** A registration page with three buttons converts worse than one with one.

**No follow-up for no-shows.** Roughly half the registrants will not attend. They need a different email from the attendees, and it should not pretend they were there.

## The judgment call

Write the promise yourself and let AI write everything that supports it. A funnel is a chain of small promises, and the first one sets the tone for the rest. If the promise is honest and specific, the copy almost writes itself. If it is vague, no amount of generated polish will save the show-up rate.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What copy does a webinar funnel need?

A registration page, three reminder emails, a scripted open and close, and a replay email. The registration promise is the piece that decides everything downstream.

### How many reminder emails should I send?

Three, each with a different job: the agenda 24 hours out, the join link an hour out, and a single line ten minutes before. None of them should sell.

### Should I test headlines or promises?

Promises. A headline test is cheap and tells you little; running two promises and comparing show-up rate and offer conversion tells you what your audience actually wants.
